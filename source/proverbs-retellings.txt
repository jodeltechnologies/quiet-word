1:1-33|Wisdom Calls Above the Crowd|Learning begins with the fear of the Lord|Proverbs opens by naming Solomon, David's son and king of Israel, and explaining why these sayings have been gathered. They teach wisdom, justice, fair judgement and discretion. Young people need this instruction, and people already considered wise must keep listening. The foundation is the fear of the Lord. Knowledge begins with reverence for God and a willingness to receive correction. A son is therefore urged to hear both his father's teaching and his mother's instruction. Their counsel is pictured as an ornament of honour, something worth carrying into public life rather than leaving behind at home.~The first temptation comes through companions who offer belonging and quick wealth. They propose an attack on innocent people, confident that their victims can be swallowed up and their possessions divided among the group. Their invitation to share one purse hides the violence required to fill it. The father tells his son to keep his feet away from their path. Greed has made these men unable to see the trap they are preparing for themselves. They expect to seize another person's life, yet their own lives are endangered by the course they have chosen.~Another voice then rises in the streets. Wisdom is presented as a woman calling openly at busy places and city gates. Her instruction is available where ordinary choices are made. She asks how long the inexperienced will cling to their inexperience, mockers enjoy scorn and fools hate knowledge. She offers understanding to those who turn at her rebuke. The seriousness of the warning comes from repeated refusal. People have ignored her outstretched hand, dismissed her counsel and rejected correction until the consequences of their choices arrive like a storm.~Wisdom describes a time when a late appeal cannot undo the harvest already growing from those refusals. The laughter in this poetic warning expresses the terrible reversal of people who mocked counsel and then discover that their confidence was empty. It gives readers no permission to enjoy another person's distress. Those who despised the fear of the Lord eat the fruit of their own way. Those who listen are invited into a different security. The chapter places the young listener between two calls, the companions promising stolen gain and wisdom offering a teachable life. The decision begins before the violent plan succeeds or disaster becomes visible.
