1:1-17|A Household Reports a Division|The church belongs to Christ|Paul writes as an apostle called by the will of God, and he names Sosthenes as a brother alongside him. He addresses the church of God in Corinth as people set apart in Christ Jesus and called to be holy. Their calling joins them to all those who call on the name of Jesus Christ in other places. The first thing Paul wishes them is grace and peace from God their Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. Before he asks the Corinthians to change anything, he reminds them who has called them and how widely that calling is shared.~Paul thanks God for the grace the church has already received. In Christ they have been enriched in speech and knowledge, and the testimony about Christ has been confirmed among them. They lack no spiritual gift as they wait for the Lord to be revealed. Paul is confident that Christ will sustain them to the end so they will stand blameless on his day. He grounds that confidence in the faithfulness of God, who called them into fellowship with his Son. Their gifts and their future both give him reason to thank God.~Then Paul appeals to them in the name of that same Lord. He asks them to agree with one another and put an end to the divisions among them. People from Chloe's household have told him about their quarrels. Some claim Paul as their leader, others Apollos or Cephas, and others say they belong to Christ. Paul asks whether Christ has been divided, whether Paul was crucified for them and whether they were baptised in Paul's name. Each question takes them back to something they received together.~Paul remembers baptising Crispus and Gaius and then adds the household of Stephanas. Beyond those he cannot recall whether he baptised anyone else. He is grateful that nobody can use his baptising them to say they were baptised in his name. Christ sent him to preach the gospel. Paul says he was to do so without resting the message on eloquence, lest the cross of Christ be emptied of its power. He has answered their quarrel about names by asking them to remember whose name they called on and whose death brought them together.|Paul can give thanks for a congregation and still name a damaging habit plainly. He asks where the church's allegiance belongs before addressing its factions.|Which of Paul's questions exposes the weakness of building a party around a leader?
1:18-31|The Word of the Cross|Where boasting comes to an end|Paul has just spoken of the danger of emptying the cross of its power by relying on eloquence. Now he considers what people hear when someone speaks about the cross. To those who are perishing the message sounds foolish. To those being saved it is God's power. Paul recalls the Scripture in which God promises to bring human wisdom to nothing. Then he asks where the wise person, the scribe and the debater of this age stand. What can their recognised skill accomplish when it comes to knowing God?~In God's wisdom, Paul says, the world failed to know God through its wisdom. God was pleased to save those who believe through the message that people judged foolish. Paul names two expectations his readers would recognise. Jews ask for signs, while Greeks look for wisdom. What Paul and his fellow workers actually proclaim is Christ crucified. This comes as a stumbling block to some and as folly to others. To those God calls, Jews and Greeks alike, Christ is God's power and God's wisdom. What people call God's foolishness is wiser than they are, and what they call his weakness is stronger than they are.~Paul then asks the Corinthians to look around at the people who received that call. Few were regarded as wise by human standards. Few had influence, and few were from notable families. God chose what the world counted foolish to shame the wise and what it counted weak to shame the strong. He chose what the world held low and despised, things people hardly counted at all, to bring to nothing the things they thought mattered. Their own congregation provides evidence of what Paul has been saying about the way God works.~The result is that nobody has grounds to boast before God. It is because of God that they are in Christ Jesus. Christ has become wisdom for them from God, as well as their righteousness, holiness and redemption. Paul finishes with the scriptural instruction that anyone who boasts should boast in the Lord. The Corinthians have been measuring themselves by names and distinctions that people in their city value. Paul brings them back to the one in whom their whole standing before God is found.|Paul connects the message of the cross with the kind of people God calls. Together they question the church's confidence in social status and persuasive display.|Why does Paul ask the Corinthians to consider who they were when they were called?
2:1-16|What the Spirit Makes Known|Depending on God in teaching|Paul asks the Corinthians to remember his first visit. He did not arrive with an impressive display of speech or wisdom. He had resolved to centre his teaching among them on Jesus Christ and his crucifixion. Paul recalls being with them in weakness, fear and much trembling. His message did not depend on persuasive arguments devised to display the speaker's skill. It came with a demonstration of the Spirit and power. He wanted the faith of the people listening to rest on God's power, where it could stand after he had gone.~Paul does speak wisdom among the mature. He describes it as God's wisdom hidden in a mystery, which God determined before the ages for their glory. The rulers of this age did not understand it. Had they understood, they would never have crucified the Lord of glory. Paul draws on Scripture to speak of things no eye has seen, no ear has heard and no human heart has imagined, which God has prepared for those who love him. God has now revealed these things through his Spirit.~The Spirit searches everything, even the depths of God. To help his readers grasp what that means, Paul starts with the inner life they know. A person's own spirit knows what goes on within that person. In a similar way it is God's Spirit who knows what belongs to God. The believers have received the Spirit from God so that they may understand the things God has freely given them. Paul speaks about these things in words taught by the Spirit, fitting spiritual words to spiritual truths.~Someone who receives only what natural understanding can take in regards the things of God's Spirit as foolish and cannot recognise them for what they are. They are discerned spiritually. The spiritual person can discern these things and is not adequately judged by someone without that understanding. Paul takes his readers back to a question from Scripture about who has known the Lord's mind well enough to instruct him. Then he says that we have the mind of Christ. That is the answer to the question of where their knowledge comes from, and it gives them reason for humility about what they have received.|Paul cares about what supports faith after a speaker leaves. The Spirit's work gives teachers and hearers a reason to approach spiritual matters with humility.|What did Paul want their faith to rest on?
3:1-23|The Field and the Building|Working together under God|Paul returns to the way he found the Corinthians able to receive his teaching. He had to speak to them as infants in Christ and give them milk because solid food was more than they could take. He says they are still unready, and he names what makes that plain. Jealousy and quarrelling continue among them. One person follows Paul and another follows Apollos. Their treatment of the two men shows how much their thinking is still shaped by ordinary rivalry.~What is Apollos, Paul asks, and what is Paul? They are servants through whom the Corinthians came to believe, each doing what the Lord assigned him. Paul planted and Apollos watered. God gave the growth. The person planting and the person watering belong to the same work. Each will receive according to his own labour. Paul calls the Corinthians God's field and God's building. The workmen have real duties, and the field belongs to the one who gives it life.~Paul then considers the building. By the grace God gave him he laid a foundation as a skilled builder. Someone else is building on it, and each person must take care how he builds. The foundation is Jesus Christ. People may build with gold, silver and precious stones or with wood, hay and straw. The coming day will show the quality of the work as fire tests it. Work that survives brings a reward. The person whose work burns suffers loss, though he himself is saved as one passing through fire. Paul then reminds the whole congregation that together they are God's temple and that God's Spirit dwells among them. God takes seriously what anyone does to destroy that temple.~Paul returns to the danger of thinking oneself wise by the standards of the age. He cites Scripture about God catching the wise in their craftiness and knowing how empty their thoughts can be. Then he asks the Corinthians to stop boasting about human leaders. Paul, Apollos and Cephas are all theirs, along with the world, life and death, the present and what is to come. They belong to Christ, and Christ belongs to God. The Corinthians began by trying to decide which teacher they belonged to. Paul turns their attention to the one to whom the whole church belongs.|The field image gives distinct tasks a place in one work. The building image asks people to consider the quality of what they add to the foundation.|What was within Paul's and Apollos's responsibility, and what belonged to God?
4:1-21|A Steward Answers to His Master|Faithfulness when opinions differ|Paul has told the Corinthians that their teachers are there for their good and that they belong to Christ. He now tells them how to regard those teachers. They are servants of Christ and stewards of the mysteries of God. What matters in a steward is faithfulness to the one who entrusted him with the work. Paul takes the verdict of a human court lightly in comparison with the Lord's. Even his own clear conscience cannot acquit him. The Lord is his judge. When the Lord comes, he will bring hidden things to light and disclose the purposes of people's hearts. Then each will receive whatever praise is due from God.~Paul says he has applied these things to himself and Apollos for their benefit. They are to learn from the example to stay within what is written and stop taking pride in one teacher over another. He asks what they have that they did not receive. If they received it, what ground do they have for boasting as though they had made it theirs? He speaks about their satisfaction with a sharp irony. They act as if they are already rich and reigning, and Paul says he wishes they really were reigning so the apostles might reign with them.~The apostles' actual circumstances make the irony plain. Paul pictures them at the end of a procession, like men condemned to die and made a spectacle to the world. The Corinthians consider themselves wise, strong and honoured. The apostles go hungry and thirsty, are poorly clothed and have no settled home. They work with their own hands. When insulted they bless, when persecuted they endure, and when slandered they answer kindly. They are treated as refuse.~Paul writes to warn the Corinthians as his beloved children. They might have many guardians in Christ, but through the gospel he became their father. He urges them to imitate him and has sent Timothy to remind them of his way of life in Christ, the same way he teaches in every church. Some have grown arrogant as if Paul would never come again. If the Lord wills, he says, he will come soon and find out what power lies behind their talk. God's kingdom is a matter of power. He ends by asking whether they want him to come with a rod or with love and a spirit of gentleness.|Paul tells the church to hold its verdicts with restraint. He also gives an example people can examine in his life and in Timothy's account of it.|What does Paul say the Lord will bring to light when he comes?
5:1-13|A Case the Church Had Tolerated|Facing harm within the fellowship|A report has reached Paul about sexual immorality in the church. A man is living with his father's wife, conduct Paul says is not tolerated even among the Gentiles. He is disturbed by the act and by the congregation's response to it. The Corinthians have remained proud when they should have mourned and removed the man from their fellowship. Paul has just asked how they wish him to come to them. Here they can see the kind of matter that requires them to take his warning seriously.~Paul is away from Corinth, yet he says he is present in spirit and has already passed judgment on the case. When they assemble in the name of the Lord Jesus, they are to act with the Lord's power. Paul speaks of handing the man over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that his spirit may be saved on the day of the Lord. The action he calls for is severe. He gives it an aim beyond the immediate separation, the man's salvation on the Lord's day.~To show why the congregation's response matters, Paul asks them to think about dough. A little leaven works through the whole batch. They are to clear out the old leaven and be a fresh batch, in keeping with what they already are. Christ, their Passover sacrifice, has been offered. The fitting way to keep the feast is with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth. Malice and wickedness belong to the leaven that must go. Paul has moved from a single reported act to the habits the church allows to spread through its common life.~Paul clarifies an earlier instruction about keeping company with sexually immoral people. Avoiding every immoral, greedy or dishonest person in the world would require leaving the world. He is speaking about someone who bears the name of brother and persists in sexual immorality, greed, idolatry, abusive speech, drunkenness or swindling. Paul tells them to withhold even the fellowship of a meal from such a person. God judges those outside. The church has responsibility for those within, and Paul ends by telling the congregation to remove the wrongdoer from among them.|The leaven illustration makes the congregation's response part of the issue. Paul treats shared responsibility and holiness as matters that require action.|How does Paul explain the scope of the judgement he asks the church to make?
6:1-20|Disputes and the Use of the Body|Belonging to Christ in everyday decisions|Paul turns to disputes between believers. He asks how one of them dares take a case against another before the unrighteous instead of bringing it to the saints. The saints will judge the world, he says, and even angels. Surely there should be someone among them able to settle ordinary affairs between brothers. Instead one believer brings another before unbelievers. The existence of these lawsuits is already a defeat. Paul asks why they would not rather suffer a wrong or bear a loss. As it stands, they are wronging and defrauding their own brothers and sisters.~Paul warns that the unrighteous will not inherit God's kingdom. His examples include sexual immorality, idolatry, adultery, theft, greed, drunkenness, abusive speech and fraud. Some of the Corinthians had lived in these ways. Paul reminds them that they were washed, made holy and justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by God's Spirit. He brings those words into an argument about what they do with one another now. Their former conduct gives them a way to recognise the things from which they were delivered.~Then Paul answers a claim about freedom. Even where something is permissible, it may be unhelpful, and Paul refuses to come under its power. Food is for the stomach and the stomach for food, and God will bring both to an end. The body is for the Lord, and the Lord for the body. God raised the Lord and will raise believers by his power. Paul asks them to consider what it means that their bodies are members of Christ. Joining oneself to a prostitute makes the two one body, as Scripture says. The person joined to the Lord is one spirit with him.~Paul tells them to flee sexual immorality. He says this sin is committed against one's own body, and then asks whether they know what that body has become. It is a temple of the Holy Spirit, whom they have received from God. They belong to God because they were bought at a price. That is why they are to glorify God in their bodies. The chapter began with people claiming what was owed to them in a dispute. It ends with a reminder of the price already paid for them and the obligation that follows.|The chapter puts the rights people claim alongside the obligations of belonging to Christ. Paul asks whether a choice serves God's glory in the body and in the fellowship.|How does belonging to Christ change Paul's answer to the claim that something is permissible?
7:1-24|Households and Their Different Callings|Faithfulness in present responsibilities|The Corinthians had written to Paul about relations between men and women. He begins his answer with married people and speaks to husband and wife in the same terms. Each owes the other the affection that belongs to marriage, and each has a claim on the other. If they set aside their ordinary relations for a time of prayer, it should be something they agree to together, with a time to come together again. Paul knows that self-control varies from person to person. He wishes others could live as he does, yet he recognises that one person receives one gift from God and another receives a different gift.~He then speaks to the unmarried and to widows. Remaining single, as he has, is a good course for those who can do it. Those who find that the struggle with desire overwhelms them should marry. When he addresses couples already married, he appeals to the Lord's command against separation and divorce. He tells a wife who has separated to remain unmarried or be reconciled to her husband, and tells a husband to keep his marriage. A new conviction about what is spiritually desirable gives neither spouse permission to cast off the other.~A different question arises where one spouse believes and the other does not. Paul gives his own counsel for this case. If the unbelieving husband or wife is willing to stay, the believer should remain in the marriage. Paul speaks of the unbelieving spouse as sanctified in relation to the believer, and of their children as holy. He also leaves their ultimate salvation open. How can either spouse know whether the other will be saved through them? If the unbelieving spouse chooses to leave, Paul says to let the separation happen. God has called believers to peace.~Paul extends the principle to other conditions in which people first heard God's call. The circumcised and the uncircumcised need not change their outward status. Keeping God's commandments is the matter before them both. A slave called in the Lord is the Lord's freed person, and a free person called by Christ becomes Christ's servant. Paul speaks to the person who can gain freedom and reminds everyone that they were bought with a price. The household and the wider world may assign very different places to people. Each can begin to live with God in the place where the call reached them.|Paul applies the call of God to concrete situations. Faith can be practised within existing obligations while room remains for changes the passage permits.|What does Paul say about a believing spouse whose unbelieving spouse is willing to remain?
7:25-40|Choices in a Time of Distress|Making decisions with devotion in view|When Paul turns to those who have never married, he says plainly that he has no command from the Lord on their particular question. He gives a judgement as someone whom the Lord has shown mercy and made trustworthy. The present distress weighs in his advice. A person bound to a wife should stay with her, and a person free from that bond need not seek a wife. Anyone who marries has committed no sin. Paul expects some who marry to face trouble in ordinary life, and he would spare them that trouble where he can. These sentences explain both the seriousness of his counsel and the room he leaves for a choice.~His thoughts then turn to the shortness of the time. The married, the grieving and the joyful all have present concerns that can absorb their attention. A person may buy something and feel settled by what it adds to the household. Another may be so occupied with sorrow that tomorrow seems impossible to imagine. Paul names these conditions together and asks his readers to hold them in view of the passing form of this world. They still have marriages to care for, griefs to bear and purchases to make. Each of those things belongs within a life whose horizon reaches beyond the present order.~Paul explains why he values singleness in these circumstances. An unmarried man can give his attention to the things of the Lord, while a married man also has responsibilities to his wife. The same distinction applies to a woman devoted to the Lord who has no husband and a wife whose life includes caring for her husband. Paul says that he gives this advice for their benefit. He wants them to attend to the Lord with as little distraction as possible, and he takes care to leave them freedom to act.~He considers a case where someone believes marriage is the proper course and says to let it take place. A settled decision against marriage can also be honoured when it is made freely and carried through with self-control. Then he speaks of a widow. She is bound while her husband lives, and after his death she is free to marry whom she wishes, only in the Lord. Paul thinks she will be happier if she remains as she is. He gives that preference as his judgement, confident that he too has the Spirit of God.|Paul explains what makes his counsel fitting to the situation. His concern is that people can attend to the Lord while facing the responsibilities of the choice they make.|What reason does Paul give for the advice he offers in this part of the chapter?
8:1-13|A Meal That Could Wound a Conscience|Love governs what knowledge permits|The Corinthians bring Paul a question about food that has been offered to idols. Some of them possess an answer they consider sufficient. An idol has no real standing as a god, and there is one God. Paul shares that conviction. He speaks of one God, the Father, from whom all things come and for whom believers live, and of one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom all things came and through whom they live. Before he draws out what follows for their meals, he asks how they are using this knowledge. A person can grow proud of having the right answer. Love builds up the people around that person.~Some members of the fellowship still associate the food with the worship in which it was offered. Habit and memory are part of what they bring to a meal. When they eat that food as something belonging to an idol, their conscience is troubled and defiled. Paul makes clear that food itself gives no one a better standing before God. Those who leave it alone lose nothing before him, and those who eat gain nothing before him. That should make it possible for a person with freedom about food to pause and consider the person whose experience is different.~Paul gives the Corinthians a scene they can picture. Someone with knowledge sits down to eat in an idol's temple. A person with a weak conscience sees the believer there and takes courage from the example. The spectator goes ahead and eats although the act still means participation in idol worship to that person. The confident believer may imagine that the example has helped a brother or sister gain freedom. Paul sees a person drawn into doing what conscience still condemns. A meal has become the occasion for the ruin of someone for whom Christ died.~That last description governs Paul's answer. The person at risk belongs to Christ, who gave his life for that person. When believers wound the weak conscience of a brother or sister, they sin against Christ himself. Paul applies the answer first to his own table. If food causes his brother to stumble, he says he will give up eating meat rather than cause the fall. The question that began with the status of an idol has reached the duty of the person whose example another believer is likely to follow.|Knowledge can describe what a person is free to do. 9:1-18|A Right Paul Chooses to Lay Aside|Serving the gospel ahead of advantage|Paul asks the Corinthians to think about what they already know of his work among them. He has seen Jesus their Lord, and the congregation itself is evidence of his apostleship. Others may question his claim, yet the Corinthians have seen the fruit of his labour first-hand. As he answers those who examine him, he asks whether he and his fellow workers have the right to food and drink and to travel with a believing wife, as other apostles, the Lord's brothers and Cephas do. He asks why he and Barnabas should be treated as the only ones obliged to earn their own living while they carry out this work.~The first examples come from everyday occupations. A soldier does not pay the costs of serving in the army. Someone who plants a vineyard can expect to eat its fruit. A shepherd drinks milk from the flock he tends. Then Paul goes to the law of Moses. An ox treading grain is to be allowed to eat as it works. Paul draws from that command a principle for the ploughman and the thresher, who both labour in hope of sharing in the harvest. If he and his fellow workers have sown spiritual things among the Corinthians, a share in their material goods is a reasonable return.~Paul takes the argument further. Other workers already receive support from this congregation. Those who serve at the temple eat from what is brought there, and those who attend the altar share in the offerings. The Lord likewise directed those who proclaim the gospel to live from that work. Paul piles up the examples so the Corinthians can see the right clearly. Then he tells them what he has done with it. He and his companions have endured the cost themselves so that they would place no obstacle in the way of the gospel of Christ.~Paul has no intention of turning this letter into a request for payment. He feels under necessity to preach, and he says that woe awaits him if he fails to do it. The work was entrusted to him as a stewardship. What reward, then, can he speak of as his own? He can give the gospel freely as he preaches it and leave unused the right that belongs to him in this work. His choice has meaning because the right is real and others may properly exercise it.|Paul can recognise a genuine right and still decide that its exercise would hinder his work in a particular setting. 9:19-27|The Runner and the Finish|Discipline for the sake of others|Paul has just explained why he can preach without taking the support due to him. He now describes the wider choice that lies beneath it. As a free man he owes no one the service of a slave, yet he has made himself a servant to all so that he may win more people. Among Jews he lives as a Jew. When he is with people under the law, he can order his conduct as they do so that his own habits do not get in the way of their hearing. He makes this choice even though he does not count himself under the law in the same way.~He can also live among people who are outside the law of Moses without insisting that their customs become Jewish before he speaks with them. Paul marks the boundary of that freedom. He is answerable to God and under the law of Christ. Then he names another group whom it would be easy to leave behind. With the weak he becomes weak, so that he may win the weak. He does them for the sake of the gospel and hopes to share in its blessings. The responsibility is his as well as theirs. To show how seriously he takes it, Paul asks his readers to picture runners in a race. All run, and one receives the prize. He wants them to run with the purpose of obtaining it. Competitors practise self-control in all things for a crown that will perish. Paul thinks of a crown that will last and of the training appropriate to that hope.~The picture changes from a runner to a boxer. Paul gives each movement an aim, like a fighter who strikes with a particular object in view. He disciplines his own body and brings it under control. After calling others to run, he wants to be fit to finish the course himself. The freedom to change his habits for the sake of his hearers and the discipline to govern his own conduct are part of the same work. Both have their place in the life of a person who wants others to receive the gospel he preaches.|Paul's flexibility has a purpose and boundaries.
