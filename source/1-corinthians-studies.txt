1:1-17|A Household Reports a Division|The church belongs to Christ|Paul thanks God for the gifts of the church at Corinth. Then he says people from Chloe's household have reported quarrels. Some claim Paul, Apollos, Cephas or Christ as their party. He asks whether Christ is divided and whether Paul was crucified for them. He remembers the few people he baptised and returns to his commission to preach.|Paul can give thanks for a congregation and still name a damaging habit plainly. He asks where the church's allegiance belongs before addressing its factions.|Which of Paul's questions exposes the weakness of building a party around a leader?
1:18-31|The Word of the Cross|Where boasting comes to an end|Paul says the message of the cross appears foolish to some and is God's power to those being saved. He describes different demands for signs and wisdom, then points to Christ crucified. He asks the Corinthians to consider their calling: many lacked the standing that attracts public honour. God gives them reason to boast in the Lord.|Paul connects the message of the cross with the kind of people God calls. Together they question the church's confidence in social status and persuasive display.|Why does Paul ask the Corinthians to consider who they were when they were called?
2:1-16|What the Spirit Makes Known|Depending on God in teaching|Paul recalls coming in weakness and concentrating on Jesus Christ crucified. He wanted the Corinthians' faith to rest in God's power. He describes wisdom God reveals through the Spirit, who searches even God's deep things. Those who have received the Spirit can understand things freely given by God.|Paul cares about what supports faith after a speaker leaves. The Spirit's work gives teachers and hearers a reason to approach spiritual matters with humility.|What did Paul want their faith to rest on?
3:1-23|The Field and the Building|Working together under God|Paul says jealousy and factions show the Corinthians' immaturity. He planted, Apollos watered and God gave growth. He then compares the church to a building with Jesus Christ as its foundation. Each builder's work will be tested. The community is God's temple, and no one should boast in human leaders.|The field image gives distinct tasks a place in one work. The building image asks people to consider the quality of what they add to the foundation.|What was within Paul's and Apollos's responsibility, and what belonged to God?
4:1-21|A Steward Answers to His Master|Faithfulness when opinions differ|Paul says ministers should be regarded as servants entrusted with God's mysteries. A steward must be faithful, and the Lord will disclose hidden motives. Paul contrasts the Corinthians' satisfaction with the hardships of the apostles. As a father in the gospel, he warns them, sends Timothy to remind them of his ways and prepares to visit.|Paul tells the church to hold its verdicts with restraint. He also gives an example people can examine in his life and in Timothy's account of it.|What does Paul say the Lord will bring to light when he comes?
5:1-13|A Case the Church Had Tolerated|Facing harm within the fellowship|Paul addresses a case of sexual immorality the congregation has allowed while remaining proud. He calls them to act and uses the image of leaven spreading through dough. He clarifies a former instruction about fellowship, distinguishing the church's responsibility for its members from God's judgement of outsiders.|The leaven illustration makes the congregation's response part of the issue. Paul treats shared responsibility and holiness as matters that require action.|How does Paul explain the scope of the judgement he asks the church to make?
6:1-20|Disputes and the Use of the Body|Belonging to Christ in everyday decisions|Paul challenges believers who take disputes with one another before outside courts. He asks whether they would accept a wrong and recalls the change God has made in them. He turns to sexual conduct and describes the body as belonging to the Lord and as a temple of the Holy Spirit. They were bought at a price.|The chapter puts the rights people claim alongside the obligations of belonging to Christ. Paul asks whether a choice serves God's glory in the body and in the fellowship.|How does belonging to Christ change Paul's answer to the claim that something is permissible?
7:1-24|Households and Their Different Callings|Faithfulness in present responsibilities|Paul answers questions about marriage, singleness and mixed households. He speaks of mutual duties and makes room for different gifts. Where an unbelieving spouse consents to remain, the believer should not dismiss the spouse. He then considers circumcision and slavery as circumstances in which people may have been called.|Paul applies the call of God to concrete situations. Faith can be practised within existing obligations while room remains for changes the passage permits.|What does Paul say about a believing spouse whose unbelieving spouse is willing to remain?
7:25-40|Choices in a Time of Distress|Making decisions with devotion in view|Paul offers a judgement about unmarried people in view of the present distress and the shortness of time. He considers the real concerns that marriage brings and the opportunity for undivided attention to the Lord. He distinguishes his advice from a command, gives people room to marry and addresses a widow's freedom to marry in the Lord.|Paul explains what makes his counsel fitting to the situation. His concern is that people can attend to the Lord while facing the responsibilities of the choice they make.|What reason does Paul give for the advice he offers in this part of the chapter?
8:1-13|A Meal That Could Wound a Conscience|Love governs what knowledge permits|The Corinthians ask about food offered to idols. Paul affirms that there is one God, while recognising that some believers still associate the food with their former worship. An example set by someone confident may lead another to act against conscience. Paul is willing to give up meat if it causes a brother to stumble.|Knowledge can describe what a person is free to do. Love also considers what someone else is likely to learn from watching that choice.|Why does Paul give weight to the conscience of the person who sees another believer eating?
9:1-18|A Right Paul Chooses to Lay Aside|Serving the gospel ahead of advantage|Paul defends his apostleship and argues that workers have a right to material support. He uses examples from military service, farming, the law and temple service. He says the Lord has provided for gospel workers to live from their work. Paul has chosen to forego the right in Corinth so that he will place no obstacle in the way of the gospel.|Paul can recognise a genuine right and still decide that its exercise would hinder his work in a particular setting. The reason for restraint is the good of the people hearing the gospel.|What right is Paul defending, and why does he choose to set it aside?
9:19-27|The Runner and the Finish|Discipline for the sake of others|Though free, Paul makes himself a servant to many. He describes adapting his approach to Jews, Gentiles and the weak so that he might win people. He compares the life to a runner and a boxer who trains deliberately. He applies the discipline to himself as someone who preaches to others.|Paul's flexibility has a purpose and boundaries. He exercises restraint in his own conduct so that his life does not contradict the message he gives.|What does the athlete image add to Paul's explanation of becoming a servant to many?
10:1-22|Lessons From Israel in the Wilderness|Receiving privileges with humility|Paul recalls Israel passing through the sea, eating and drinking in the wilderness. He names instances of craving, idolatry, immorality, testing and grumbling. The accounts warn people who think they stand securely. God is faithful in temptation and provides a way to endure. Paul urges the church to flee idolatry and considers what participation at a table means.|Shared privileges gave Israel real gifts and still left room for disobedience. Paul asks believers to learn from the old events before overestimating their own strength.|How does Paul place God's faithfulness beside the warning against confidence in oneself?
10:23-33|At Someone Else's Table|Seeking another person's good|Paul discusses meat sold in the market and food served by an unbelieving host. He gives room to eat without an inquiry, then addresses the case where someone explicitly identifies the food as an offering. He asks hearers to consider conscience, seek another's good and do ordinary things such as eating and drinking to God's glory.|The same food can be received in one circumstance and declined in another. Paul directs his readers to attend to the people present and the meaning the action has for them.|What new information at a meal changes the decision Paul gives?
11:1-16|Honour in Prayer and Prophecy|Considering others in public worship|Paul commends the holding of traditions and discusses honour, head coverings and the conduct of men and women who pray or prophesy. He reasons from creation and from the way their actions are understood. He also says that man and woman are interdependent in the Lord and that all things are from God.|This passage asks worshippers to consider the honour their conduct communicates. Paul's reminder of mutual dependence gives humility a place in the discussion.|How does the statement that all things are from God shape the way you read the surrounding instructions?
11:17-34|When One Eats and Another Goes Hungry|Recognising the body at the Lord's Supper|Paul hears that divisions show themselves when the church gathers to eat. Some are hungry while others have gone ahead. He recounts what he received about Jesus giving the bread and cup and calls people to examine themselves. He asks them to wait for one another when they come together.|The meal proclaims Jesus' death while the congregation's conduct can humiliate people with little to bring. Paul addresses the way they treat one another as part of taking the supper seriously.|What change does Paul give the church for the next time it gathers to eat?
12:1-31|Many Members in One Body|Giving honour to the overlooked|Paul names different gifts, forms of service and activities that come from the same Spirit, Lord and God. He compares the church to a body. A foot belongs even if it is not a hand, and an eye cannot dismiss the hand. Members regarded as weaker are necessary. When one suffers or is honoured, all share in it.|The body image questions both the person who feels dispensable and the person who thinks others are dispensable. God gives each member a place in a shared life.|Who might be overlooked in your fellowship, and how does Paul's picture change the way you see that person's contribution?
13:1-13|The More Excellent Way|Love gives gifts their purpose|Paul imagines impressive speech, knowledge, faith, giving and sacrifice without love. He then describes love through its patience, kindness, restraint and endurance. Gifts such as prophecy and partial knowledge have their appointed limit. Faith, hope and love remain, and Paul calls love the greatest.|Paul measures gifts by the character in which they are exercised. Love becomes visible in how people respond to irritation, wrongdoing and partial understanding.|Which description of love would most change the way you exercise a gift you already have?
14:1-25|Words People Can Understand|Using gifts to build up hearers|Paul tells the church to pursue love and desire spiritual gifts. He distinguishes speaking in a tongue from speech that the congregation can understand and receive. Using musical sounds and the example of prayer, he asks how people can respond to words they cannot follow. He also considers what a visitor hears in the gathering.|A person using a gift should consider the one trying to listen. Interpretation and intelligible speech help other people take part rather than remain observers.|How does Paul's example of someone saying Amen show why understanding matters?
14:26-40|Room for Others to Speak|Order that serves the congregation|Paul pictures people arriving with a psalm, teaching, revelation or a tongue. He sets limits, calls for interpretation and asks others to weigh what prophets say. He addresses speech and silence in the gathered church. His governing instruction is that everything should build up the church and be done decently and in order.|Order makes room for contributions to be heard and tested. Paul asks people with something to offer to consider the benefit of the whole gathering.|Which instructions in the passage help a gathering receive and evaluate what someone says?
15:1-19|The Gospel Paul Received and Passed On|Faith rests on a risen Christ|Paul recalls the message he delivered: Christ died for sins, was buried, rose and appeared to many, including Paul. He describes grace at work in his own calling after persecuting the church. Then he asks what follows if the dead are never raised. If Christ is still dead, their faith and proclamation are empty.|Paul places his own experience inside a testimony others can also give. The resurrection is part of what the gospel announces and of what believers trust.|What would be lost from Paul's message if the claim that Christ rose were removed?
15:20-34|The Firstfruits and the Last Enemy|Hope that changes present conduct|Paul calls the risen Christ the firstfruits of those who have died. He relates Christ's resurrection to Adam, the order of resurrection and the defeat of every enemy, with death last. He asks why people put themselves in danger if there is no resurrection and urges the Corinthians to wake up to righteous living.|The hope Paul describes changes how sacrifice and discipline look in the present. His discussion of the future leads to an immediate call to live differently.|Why does Paul connect his exposure to danger with the truth of the resurrection?
15:35-58|A Seed Goes Into the Ground|The hope of a changed body|Paul answers questions about how the dead are raised and what body they will have. A seed is sown and God gives it a body. Paul describes decay giving way to incorruption and the mortal clothed with immortality. He closes with thanksgiving for victory through Jesus and urges steady work in the Lord.|Paul's image gives continuity and change a place in the same hope. The chapter ends by giving ordinary faithful work a reason to continue.|How does the resurrection hope support Paul's final instruction about work in the Lord?
16:1-24|Setting Something Aside and Greeting Fellow Workers|Preparing generosity in advance|Paul gives directions for the collection, asking people to set something aside weekly as they prosper. He explains his travel plans, asks the church to receive Timothy without fear and mentions Apollos. He remembers people devoted to serving believers and sends greetings. He tells the church to do everything in love.|Advance preparation gives generosity a dependable form. The closing greetings also ask the church to notice the people who carry its work.|Why does Paul ask people to put something aside before he arrives?
