1:1-17|Paul Explains Why He Wants to Come to Rome|A gospel meant for every hearer|Paul introduces himself as a servant of Jesus Christ, called to be an apostle and set apart for God's gospel. This good news had been promised beforehand through the prophets in the Scriptures. It concerns God's Son, descended from David according to the flesh and declared Son of God in power through his resurrection from the dead. Through Jesus, Paul says, he received grace and his commission to call people among all nations to the obedience that comes from faith. The believers in Rome belong among those called to Jesus Christ.~Paul addresses them as people loved by God and called to be saints. He wishes them grace and peace from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. Before he tells them why he is writing, he thanks God through Jesus for them. Their faith is being spoken of widely. Paul remembers them continually in prayer and asks that, by God's will, a way may finally open for him to visit them.~He longs to see them in person and share a spiritual gift that will strengthen them. He goes on to put the visit in terms of what he hopes to receive as well. Their faith and his would encourage each other. He has intended to come many times and has been prevented. He still hopes for fruit from his work among them, as among other Gentiles. He feels a duty to Greeks and those outside Greek culture, to the wise and the unwise.~That debt helps explain his eagerness to preach in Rome. He is unashamed of the gospel because it is God's power for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. In it God's righteousness is revealed in connection with faith. Paul gives the thought a line from Scripture that will carry into the rest of the letter. The righteous will live by faith.|Paul writes with a calling to teach and an expectation of receiving encouragement. The people he hopes to visit already have faith from which he expects to benefit.|What does Paul expect to give and receive if he visits Rome?
1:18-32|When Gratitude Gives Way to Idols|Honouring the Creator|Paul has announced the good news as God's power for salvation. Now he shows why people need it. God's wrath is revealed against ungodliness and wrongdoing, Paul says, because people hold down the truth by the way they live. God has made something of himself plain in the things he has made. His eternal power and divine nature can be seen through creation. Paul says people are therefore without excuse.~The refusal begins with worship and thanksgiving. Although people knew God, they gave him neither the honour nor the thanks due to him. Their reasoning became empty and their hearts darkened. Claiming wisdom, they traded the glory of the immortal God for images of mortal people, birds, animals and creeping things. Paul describes this as exchanging God's truth for a lie and serving created things in place of their Creator.~Paul repeatedly says that God gave them over to the course they chose. He speaks first of desires through which they dishonoured their bodies. He then describes women exchanging natural relations and men abandoning relations with women and burning in desire for one another. Finally, he says that because people saw no reason to retain the knowledge of God, they were given over to a corrupt mind and conduct that followed from it.~The final list brings Paul's argument into life with other people. He names envy, murder, quarrelling, deceit, gossip, pride, disobedience to parents and a lack of mercy, among other kinds of wrongdoing. People know God's judgment against such conduct and persist in it, Paul says. They also approve when others do the same. The passage that begins with what people can see of their Creator ends with what they have come to accept among themselves.|Paul begins the account with a refusal to honour and thank God. Gratitude helps keep the gifts of creation in relation to the one who gives them.|Which changes in conduct does Paul trace back to a change in worship?
2:1-16|The Person Who Judges Is Also Accountable|Receiving kindness as a call to repent|Paul turns from the people he has described to the reader who condemns them. The person passing judgment can recognise wrongdoing clearly enough to name it. If he practises the same things, that judgment also tells against him. God's judgment is according to truth, Paul says. Someone who sees another person's guilt and goes on in the same conduct has no reason to expect to escape it.~Paul asks whether such a person takes God's kindness, patience and willingness to wait lightly. That kindness has a purpose. It leads people toward repentance. A stubborn heart that refuses to repent is storing up wrath for the day when God's righteous judgment will be revealed. God will repay each person according to what that person has done.~Paul sets out the two outcomes. To those who patiently continue doing good and seek glory, honour and immortality, God will give eternal life. Wrath and trouble await those who reject the truth and obey unrighteousness. Paul names both Jews and Gentiles in speaking of trouble for wrongdoing and of glory, honour and peace for doing good. God judges without favouritism.~Those who sin without the law will be judged accordingly, and those who sin under the law will be judged by it. Hearing the law does not of itself make a person righteous before God. Gentiles who do what the law requires show its work written on their hearts. Their consciences bear witness, and their thoughts accuse or defend them. Paul brings even these inward matters into view as he mentions the day God will judge people's secrets through Jesus Christ.|God's patience gives time to repent. It is easy to interpret that time as approval when we are occupied with pointing out another person's faults.|Where is Paul asking his reader to turn attention after condemning someone else?
2:17-29|The Teacher Under His Own Teaching|Integrity that reaches the heart|Paul now addresses the reader who calls himself a Jew, relies on the law and takes pride in his relationship to God. Such a person has been taught to recognise what God approves. He considers himself a guide to the blind and a light to people in darkness, able to teach the foolish and instruct the young. In the law he has a form of knowledge and truth he can pass on.~Paul turns these claims into questions. Does the one teaching others also teach himself? If he preaches against stealing, does he steal? If he forbids adultery, does he commit it? If he detests idols, does he rob temples? A person can boast in the law while dishonouring God by breaking it. Paul reminds his reader of what Scripture says about God's name being blasphemed among the Gentiles because of such conduct.~Then Paul takes up circumcision, the outward sign of belonging to the covenant people. It has value if a person keeps the law. Breaking the law makes the sign count as uncircumcision. Paul asks what follows when an uncircumcised person carries out what the law requires. His obedience exposes the disobedience of the person who possesses the written law and bears the sign.~Paul ends with what God sees inwardly. The circumcision he describes takes place in the heart, by the Spirit, and reaches where a written code and a mark on the body cannot reach. The person who lives this way receives praise from God. The argument has travelled from the honour the teacher claims in public to the life God sees beneath that claim.|The responsibility to teach gives the teacher an additional reason to examine his own life. Public recognition cannot substitute for God's judgement of the heart.|Which of Paul's questions would help you examine a duty you urge on other people?
3:1-20|Every Mouth Comes to Silence|Honesty before God|Paul anticipates what his reader might ask after hearing what he has said about the law and circumcision. What advantage, then, does the Jew have? Paul answers that there is much advantage. To begin with, the Jews were entrusted with the words of God. Some had been unfaithful. Their unfaithfulness could never make God unfaithful. Paul appeals to Scripture to affirm that God is true and will be vindicated when his words are judged.~A further objection follows. If people's unrighteousness makes God's righteousness more evident, would God be unjust to judge them? Paul rejects the argument, for on those terms God could scarcely judge the world. He also rejects the claim that people should do evil so that good may result. He says some had slanderously attributed such teaching to him, and he calls their condemnation deserved.~Paul then returns to the question of whether having these privileges puts Jews in a better position before God's judgment. His charge has already included Jews and Gentiles alike under sin. He gathers several lines from Scripture to describe that condition. Nobody is righteous. No one seeks God. Their speech carries deceit and bitterness. Their paths are marked by violence and trouble, and they do not know the way of peace. There is no fear of God before their eyes.~Paul says the law speaks to those under it. Its words leave every mouth silent and the whole world accountable before God. Nobody will be justified before him by works of the law. Through the law comes knowledge of sin. The Scripture entrusted to this people has brought the argument to a point where every reader needs to hear what God will do.|A genuine privilege can make accountability deeper. The Scripture Paul cites gives people words for their condition before it gives them grounds for hope.|What does Paul say the law makes known when people stand accountable before God?
3:21-31|The Righteousness God Makes Known|Grace gives no room for boasting|Paul has brought the whole world before God with no defence to offer. Now he says God's righteousness has been made known apart from works of the law. The Law and the Prophets themselves bear witness to it. This righteousness comes through faith in Jesus Christ to all who believe. Paul gives the reason he can speak to them together. All have sinned and fall short of God's glory.~Those who are justified receive it as a gift of grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus. God put Christ forward as an atoning sacrifice through his blood, to be received by faith. In his patience God had passed over sins committed beforehand. Paul says that what God has now done shows his righteousness. He is just, and he justifies the one who has faith in Jesus.~Paul then asks what has become of boasting. It has been shut out. An arrangement based on what people achieve would leave them something to boast about, but faith gives them no such ground. His conclusion is that a person is justified by faith apart from works of the law. The argument concerns people who have spent their lives under the law as well as those who have lived outside it.~God is the God of Gentiles as well as of Jews. Since there is one God, he justifies the circumcised by faith and the uncircumcised through faith. Paul anticipates one last question. Does this faith overthrow the law? He says it upholds it. He will now go back to Abraham, whose place in Scripture lets him show how long this truth has been there.|The same grace meets people with different histories. Boasting loses its ground when acceptance depends on what God gives through Christ.|How does the conclusion about boasting follow from the way Paul describes justification?
4:1-12|Abraham Before the Sign|Trust in the God who justifies|Paul takes up Abraham, the ancestor whose story his Jewish readers know well. What did Abraham find? If he had been justified by works, he would have a ground for boasting, Paul says, though he would have none before God. Paul asks the reader to look at what Scripture actually says. Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness.~Paul asks his readers to think about the difference between wages and a gift. If someone works for a wage, the payment is counted as something owed. In Abraham's case, the righteousness was counted through faith. Paul speaks here of the person who trusts the God who justifies the ungodly. He then draws in David, who speaks of the blessedness of the person to whom God counts righteousness apart from works. David describes a person whose wrongdoing is forgiven, whose sins are covered and whose sin the Lord does not count against him.~Paul asks who can share this blessing. Is it for the circumcised alone? He looks at the order of events in Abraham's life. Abraham's faith was counted to him as righteousness while he was still uncircumcised. Circumcision came later as a sign and seal of the righteousness he already had by faith.~That order gives Abraham a place in the lives of people who have never borne his outward sign. He is the father of uncircumcised people who believe, so that righteousness may be counted to them also. He is also the father of circumcised people who follow in the footsteps of the faith he had before his circumcision. Paul makes the timing of the sign part of his answer about who may belong to this family of faith.|Paul pays attention to the timing of the sign. That sequence gives people beyond Abraham's physical descendants a place in the story of faith.|What happened before Abraham received the sign, and why is the order important to Paul?
4:13-25|The Promise Faced by an Old Man|Hope grounded in the power of God|Paul now considers the promise that Abraham and his descendants would inherit the world. It came through the righteousness of faith, apart from the law. If the inheritance depended on keeping the law, faith would be emptied and the promise would lose its force. The law brings wrath where it is broken. Paul says the promise rests on faith so that it depends on grace and is sure to all Abraham's offspring, including those who share his faith without having his people's law.~Scripture speaks of Abraham as father of many nations. Paul points to the God in whom Abraham believed, the God who gives life to the dead and calls into being things that do not exist. Abraham trusted him when the ordinary grounds for expecting a child had disappeared. He was about a hundred years old and knew the condition of his own body. He also knew that Sarah's womb was past bearing children.~Abraham went on trusting what God had spoken. He grew strong in faith, gave glory to God and was fully persuaded that God could do what he had promised. This is why his faith was counted to him as righteousness. Paul has brought the reader close enough to Abraham's circumstances to see what gave that faith its footing.~The words about righteousness being counted were written for later readers too. Righteousness will be counted to those who believe in the God who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead. Jesus was handed over for our trespasses and raised for our justification. At the close of the passage, the God who brings life out of death is the ground of hope for Paul's readers as he was for Abraham.|Abraham's trust takes the facts of his condition seriously. Its ground is what God is able to do.|What two human limitations does Paul name when he describes Abraham's faith?
5:1-11|Peace After Justification|Hope in the midst of trouble|Paul turns to what follows when a person has been justified by faith. Believers have peace with God through the Lord Jesus Christ. Through him they have gained access by faith into the grace in which they stand, and they rejoice in the hope of sharing God's glory. Paul speaks of this as a place already given to them and a hope for which they still wait.~He has room in that hope for trouble. Suffering produces endurance, endurance produces tested character, and character produces hope. This hope will hold because God's love has been poured into their hearts through the Holy Spirit he has given them. Paul then directs their attention to what that love has already done for them.~Christ died for the ungodly while they were still weak. Paul asks his readers to consider how seldom someone dies for a righteous person, though someone might dare to die for a good person. God shows his love in that Christ died for us while we were still sinners. Paul puts the death of Christ at the point in the story where people had nothing in their own condition to commend them.~Now that they have been justified by Christ's blood, Paul says, they can trust that they will be saved from wrath through him. If God reconciled them to himself through his Son's death while they were enemies, how much more will they be saved by his life now that they are reconciled? They rejoice in God through Jesus Christ, through whom they have received reconciliation. What Christ has already done gives Paul his reason for confidence about what lies ahead.|Christian hope in this passage begins with reconciliation God has given. It makes room to speak honestly about suffering while trusting the love that met people in their weakness.|How does Christ's death for sinners support Paul's confidence about what lies ahead?
5:12-21|Adam and Christ|Grace that abounds over sin|Paul now goes back to Adam to put the work of Christ in view over the length of human history. Sin came into the world through one man, and death came through sin. Death spread to all because all sinned. Sin was in the world before the law was given. Death reigned from Adam to Moses, even over people whose sin differed from Adam's breaking of a direct command. Paul calls Adam a figure of the one who was to come.~The effect of Adam's trespass helps Paul show the reach of Christ's gift. Many died through one man's trespass. God's grace and the gift that comes by the grace of Jesus Christ have abounded to many. A single trespass brought judgment and condemnation. The gift comes after many trespasses and brings justification. Paul draws attention to how much the gift has answered.~Death reigned through the one man. Paul says those who receive abundant grace and the gift of righteousness will reign in life through Jesus Christ. He brings the two accounts together again. One trespass brought condemnation for all, and one righteous act brought justification and life. Through one man's disobedience many were made sinners. Through the obedience of the one, many will be made righteous.~Paul says the law entered so that the trespass would increase. As sin increased, grace abounded all the more. Sin had reigned in death. Grace now reigns through righteousness leading to eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord. This leaves the reader with a question Paul will take up next. What does this abundance of grace mean for the way believers live now?|Paul places humanity's condition inside a larger account of what Christ has done. The gift is described as greater than the ruin it answers.|What changes when Paul moves from what entered through Adam to what comes through Christ?
6:1-14|Buried and Raised With Christ|Living in the newness of life|Paul anticipates a question about his words on abundant grace. Should believers continue in sin so that grace will increase? He rejects the thought and asks how those who died to sin can go on living in it. To explain what he means by dying, he brings his readers back to their baptism. Those baptised into Christ Jesus were baptised into his death. They were buried with him so that, as Christ was raised through the Father's glory, they too might walk in newness of life.~If they have been united with Christ in a death like his, Paul says, they will share in his resurrection. Their old self was crucified with him so that the body ruled by sin would lose its power and they would no longer serve sin as slaves. The person who has died has been freed from sin's claim. Paul expects those who died with Christ to live with him.~Christ, having risen, will never die again. Death no longer rules over him. He died to sin once for all and now lives to God. Paul asks his readers to reckon themselves in the light of what is true of Christ. They are to count themselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus. What they think about their life in him bears on what they do with it.~Paul therefore tells them to refuse sin's rule over their mortal bodies and its demands on their conduct. They are to present themselves to God as people brought from death to life and offer their bodily powers as instruments of righteousness. Sin will no longer rule them, Paul says, because they live under grace. He has brought the question about grace down to what they will do with their hands, their speech and everything else they can offer to God.|Grace gives believers reason to change whom they obey. Paul asks them to offer their actual capacities to God in the life they now live.|What would presenting one part of your daily life as an instrument of righteousness involve?
6:15-23|The Master You Obey|Freedom that bears the fruit of holiness|Paul raises a second question about grace. Since believers are under grace rather than under the law, may they sin? He rejects the idea. He asks his readers to think about what it means to present themselves to someone for obedience. They become servants of the one they obey. Sin leads to death, and obedience leads to righteousness. The freedom he has been describing has a direction that can be seen in daily conduct.~Paul thanks God as he looks back on what has changed for his readers. They once served sin. Then they obeyed from the heart the teaching to which they had been entrusted. Having been freed from sin, they became servants of righteousness. Paul says he is speaking in ordinary human terms because of their weakness. The familiar picture of service helps them see what their allegiance means.~They once placed their bodies at the service of impurity and lawlessness, and lawlessness led to more of the same. Now they are to put themselves at the service of righteousness, which leads to holiness. Paul asks them to look back at the fruit of the things they are now ashamed of. They were free from the service of righteousness at the time, but the end of those things was death.~Now they have been freed from sin and have become servants of God. Their fruit is holiness, and the end is eternal life. Paul brings the two courses to their conclusion in a sentence. Sin pays its wages in death. God gives eternal life as a gift through Jesus Christ our Lord.|Paul asks his hearers to compare where two kinds of obedience lead. Choices that look isolated can form habits that direct an entire life.|What fruit and conclusion does Paul associate with service to God?
7:1-13|The Command That Reveals Coveting|Recognising what the law brings to light|At the end of the previous passage, Paul described people who had been freed from sin and now served God. He returns to the question of the law and asks his readers to think about something a law can only do while a person lives. A married woman is bound by law to her husband while he is alive. If she belongs to another man during that time, the law calls her an adulteress. If her husband dies, the bond ends, and she is free to marry again. The point of the example is that death changes the reach of an existing claim.~In the case of Paul's readers, it is they who have died to the law through the body of Christ. They now belong to the one who was raised from the dead, so that they may bear fruit for God. Paul looks back on the former life and says sinful passions, aroused through the law, were at work in their bodies and bore fruit for death. Having died to what held them, they now serve in the new way of the Spirit. The change reaches beyond how they understand a written command. It concerns the person to whom they belong and the life their service produces.~This raises an awkward question. If the law could stir up sinful passions, was the law itself sinful? Paul rejects the idea. He takes a command that reaches into the thoughts people may keep to themselves. He says that he would not have recognised coveting if the law had not forbidden it. Sin took the command as an opportunity and produced desires of every kind. Paul describes himself as once alive apart from the law. When the command came, sin sprang to life and he died.~Even the command that held out life proved to bring death in his case. Paul says sin seized the opportunity, deceived him and killed him through the command. The law remains holy, and the command is holy, righteous and good. Did something good itself bring him death? Paul puts the responsibility on sin, which used something good to produce death. The command exposes sin in its true character. The example began with a rule whose claim ends at death and comes to rest on another question altogether, what can bring a person under sin into a life fruitful for God?|A good command can uncover a desire we had allowed to pass unnoticed. Paul distinguishes what the command shows from the sin that takes advantage of it.|Which command does Paul use to explain how he came to recognise coveting?
7:14-25|The Good I Want to Do|Taking inward conflict seriously|Paul has already called the command holy and good. He now says the law is spiritual, then puts his own condition beside it. In the first person he describes someone of flesh, sold under sin, who finds his actions hard to understand. He does the very thing he hates, while the thing he wants to do remains undone. There is a kind of agreement with the law even in that confession. By hating the wrong he does, he acknowledges that the law which forbids it is good.~Paul draws attention to sin dwelling within him. He says he finds no good thing dwelling in his flesh. The desire to do right is present, yet he cannot carry it through. He returns to the same experience from another direction. The good he wants stays out of reach, and the evil he wants to leave alone is what he actually does. His repeated description makes the gap plain. A person can see the goodness of the command, sincerely desire to obey it and still discover another power at work in his conduct.~Paul uses the word law again for the pattern he keeps finding. When he wants to do good, evil is close at hand. In his inward being he delights in God's law. In his bodily members he sees another law waging war against the law of his mind and taking him captive to sin. The image changes from a person whose actions puzzle him to a prisoner who can see what holds him. The agreement of his mind with God's law has not ended the struggle he describes.~At that point he cries out over his wretched condition and asks who will deliver him from this body of death. He answers with thanksgiving to God through Jesus Christ our Lord. The closing sentence holds both parts of his experience in view. With his mind he serves God's law, and with his flesh he serves the law of sin. Paul has followed the trouble all the way from the command he approves to the captivity he cannot break by wishing. His thanks directs the reader toward the deliverance he will take up as he begins to speak about life in the Spirit.|Honest confession can name the gap between conviction and conduct. Paul's cry places hope of deliverance with God through Jesus Christ.|What tension is expressed between what the speaker approves and what he actually does?
8:1-17|The Spirit and the Cry of Adoption|Living as children who call on the Father|Paul has just cried out for deliverance and given thanks through Jesus Christ. Now he tells the people who are in Christ Jesus that there is no condemnation for them. The law of the Spirit of life in Christ has set them free from the law of sin and death. The law could not overcome the weakness of the flesh. God acted by sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh and for sin, and judged sin in the flesh. Paul's purpose in following that thought is practical. The righteous requirement of the law is to be fulfilled in people who walk according to the Spirit.~Paul then asks his readers to consider what occupies their minds. People living according to the flesh set their minds on what the flesh wants, and people living according to the Spirit set their minds on what the Spirit desires. One course ends in death, while the mind set on the Spirit has life and peace. The mind ruled by the flesh is hostile to God and cannot submit to his law. Paul speaks that plainly before turning to the people in whom God's Spirit dwells. Anyone who lacks Christ's Spirit does not belong to him.~Paul sets the body's mortality beside the life that comes through righteousness. The God who raised Jesus from the dead will give life even to their mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in them. That promise bears on how they live before their bodies are raised. They owe the flesh no obedience. If they live by it they will die. If by the Spirit they put to death the deeds of the body, they will live. Paul says those led by God's Spirit are God's children.~He then brings them from the fear of slavery to the language of a household. They have received the Spirit of adoption, by whom they cry to God as Abba, Father. The Spirit bears witness with their own spirit that they are God's children. Children are also heirs, heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ. Paul includes suffering with Christ in the life of those who will share his glory. The believer who calls on God as Father has a place in that family even while the suffering is still real.|The Spirit leads believers into a family relationship with obligations and assurance. They can address God as Father even in circumstances that carry a cost.|How does adoption change the way believers approach God according to Paul?
8:18-39|Creation Groans and Love Holds|Hope when words are difficult|Paul has just spoken of suffering with Christ and sharing his glory. Now he weighs those two things. The sufferings of the present time are small beside the glory to come. His view widens from the believer to creation itself. Creation has been subjected to frustration and waits for God's children to be revealed, in hope of sharing their freedom from decay. Paul likens its groaning to the pains of childbirth. Believers who already have the firstfruits of the Spirit groan too, waiting for adoption in its fullness, the redemption of their bodies. They wait patiently for what they cannot yet see.~The Spirit helps them in their weakness. At times they do not know how to pray as they should, and the Spirit himself intercedes with groans beyond speech. God searches hearts and knows the mind of the Spirit, whose intercession for believers accords with God's will. Paul goes on to speak of all things working together for the good of those who love God and are called according to his purpose. They are to be conformed to the image of his Son, the firstborn among many brothers and sisters. Paul traces God's work from foreknowledge and purpose through calling and justification to glory.~He then puts several questions to his readers. If God is for them, who can stand against them? God gave up his own Son for them. They can trust him to give what they need with him. Who can make a charge stick against the people God has chosen, when God himself justifies them? Who can condemn them? Christ died, was raised and is at God's right hand interceding for them. Paul has given them two pictures of intercession in the same passage, one in their weakness and one at God's right hand.~He asks whether trouble, distress, persecution, hunger, want, danger or the sword can separate them from Christ's love. Scripture itself speaks of God's people facing death. Even in such circumstances, Paul says, they conquer through the one who loved them. He ends with what he is persuaded of. Neither death nor life, spiritual powers, the present or the future, height or depth, nor anything else in creation can separate them from God's love in Christ Jesus their Lord.|Paul gives groaning and assurance space in the same argument. Weakness in prayer is met by the Spirit's help and by confidence in God's love.|Where does Paul direct a believer who is too weak to know how to pray?
9:1-29|Paul Grieves for Israel|Mercy and the faithfulness of God|After speaking of the love from which nothing can separate believers, Paul tells the truth about a grief he cannot put aside. He says his conscience bears witness in the Holy Spirit. He carries great sorrow for his own people, the Israelites, and says he could wish himself accursed and cut off from Christ for their sake. He names what God has given them. The adoption and the glory, the covenants, the law, the worship and the promises are theirs. The patriarchs belong to them, and from their people came the Christ according to the flesh.~Has God's word failed? Paul answers by returning to the family stories in Scripture. He distinguishes between physical descent from Israel and belonging to Israel in the sense his argument now requires. Abraham had more than one son, and God said that his offspring would be named through Isaac. Paul recalls the promise that Sarah would have a son. Then he turns to Rebekah, whose two children had the same father, Isaac. Before the twins were born or had done good or evil, she was told that the elder would serve the younger. Paul connects this with God's purpose in calling and with what Scripture later says about Jacob and Esau.~Paul knows the objection this raises. Is there injustice with God? He cites God's words to Moses about showing mercy and compassion as he chooses. Mercy depends on God, beyond any human willing or effort. Paul brings in Pharaoh as another example. Scripture says God raised him up so that his power might be shown and his name proclaimed throughout the earth. Paul says God has mercy on whom he wills and hardens whom he wills.~The objection grows sharper. Why does God still find fault? Paul asks the questioner to consider the relation of clay to its potter. A potter can make vessels for different purposes out of the same lump. Paul speaks of God's patience in bearing vessels of wrath and of the riches of his glory made known to vessels of mercy. Those called include Gentiles as well as Jews. Hosea speaks of people once called 'not my people' becoming God's people. Isaiah speaks of a remnant of Israel being saved and of the Lord leaving survivors. Paul has answered the question of God's faithfulness by going back to the very Scriptures entrusted to the people for whom he grieves.|Paul's argument begins with grief for real people. His appeal to mercy calls for humility when considering God's freedom and the limits of human claims.|What privileges does Paul remember before asking how God's promise stands?
9:30-10:21|The Message Comes Near|Righteousness received by faith and proclaimed|Paul brings the argument back to a difference he wants his readers to see. Gentiles who had never pursued righteousness attained it through faith. Israel pursued a law of righteousness and failed to reach its goal, because they pursued it as though it depended on works. Paul says they stumbled over a stone, and brings together words from Isaiah about the stone set in Zion and the security of the one who believes. Then he speaks of his own prayer to God that Israel might be saved. He acknowledges their zeal for God and says it lacks knowledge. Seeking to establish their own righteousness, they failed to submit to God's righteousness. Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes.~Paul sets the righteousness described in the law beside the way the righteousness of faith speaks. He draws on Moses' language about going up or going down to obtain the word. The hearer has no need to ascend to bring Christ down or descend to bring him up from the dead. The word is already near, in the mouth and in the heart. Paul identifies it as the message of faith he proclaims. The person who confesses Jesus as Lord and believes in the heart that God raised him from the dead will be saved. The same Lord is Lord of all, rich toward everyone who calls on him.~Then Paul asks how someone comes to call on the Lord. Calling follows believing. Believing depends on hearing, and hearing depends on someone proclaiming the message. A preacher needs to be sent. Isaiah's words about the welcome given to messengers of good news belong here, and so does Isaiah's question about who believed what they heard. The message goes out, and hearers give different answers. Faith comes through hearing the word about Christ.~Paul asks whether Israel had heard and whether they had understood. 11:1-24|Unexpected Branches on an Olive Tree|Humility after being welcomed|Paul asks whether God has rejected his people. His own life gives the first answer. He too is an Israelite, descended from Abraham and from the tribe of Benjamin. He reminds his readers of Elijah, who complained to God that Israel had killed the prophets and torn down the altars. Elijah thought he alone had survived and that his life was in danger. God told him that he had kept seven thousand people who had never bowed to Baal. Paul sees a remnant in his own time too, chosen by grace. Grace would lose its meaning if the choice depended on works.~Israel, Paul says, failed to obtain what it was seeking. The chosen obtained it, and the others were hardened. He cites Scripture about unseeing eyes, unhearing ears and a table that becomes a snare. If their loss has brought riches to the world, Paul asks his readers to consider what their fullness would mean. He takes his ministry to the Gentiles seriously in part because he hopes to stir his own people and save some of them.~Paul pictures an olive tree. If the root is holy, the branches are holy. Some of the branches have been broken off, and a shoot from a wild olive has been grafted among the others and now shares the nourishment of the root. Paul tells his Gentile readers to think carefully about any claim to superiority. The root supports the grafted branch. If the branch replies that others were removed to make room for it, Paul points back to what happened. They were broken off through unbelief, and the grafted branch stands by faith. That is reason for reverence.~Paul asks them to consider God's kindness and severity. God has been kind to them, and they are to continue in that kindness. If a wild branch could be grafted into a cultivated tree, Paul says, how much more readily could the natural branches be grafted back into their own olive tree?|The image gives people included through faith a reason for gratitude and reverence. 11:25-36|The Argument Ends in Praise|A place for humility before God's wisdom|Paul tells his Gentile readers that he wants them to understand a mystery so they will have less reason to think themselves wise. He draws on Scripture about a deliverer coming from Zion, turning ungodliness away from Jacob, and God keeping his covenant by taking away sins. Paul has just spoken about branches that God can graft back into their own tree. Here he states the hope in terms of God's promise.~Paul keeps two facts in view at once. The same people who have opposed the gospel are still the people about whom Paul can speak of God's love and calling. He asks the Gentiles to remember that they too have a history of disobedience.~They once disobeyed God and have now received mercy through Israel's disobedience. Israel is now disobedient and can also receive mercy in connection with the mercy shown to the Gentiles. Paul says God has shut all up in disobedience in order to have mercy on all. Neither group, then, can take its own record of obedience as the reason it has been shown mercy. He speaks of the depth of God's riches, wisdom and knowledge. God's judgments are beyond searching out, and his ways are beyond tracing. Paul asks who has known God's mind, who has served as his adviser, or who has first given him something that God now owes back. All things come from God, hold together through him and come to their end in him. Paul gives him glory forever. 12:1-21|A Living Sacrifice in Ordinary Relationships|Love that takes visible form|Paul has spent much of the letter tracing the mercy of God. He now appeals to his readers on the strength of that mercy. He asks them to present their bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God. They are to resist being shaped by the present age and be transformed by the renewing of their minds. A mind being renewed can discern what God wants, what is good and pleasing and complete. The next thing Paul asks of that mind is that it judge its own importance soberly, according to the measure of faith God has given.~Paul pictures a body with many members. The members have different work to do, and in Christ they belong to one another. Gifts differ according to the grace each has received. Paul tells the person who prophesies to do so according to faith, the person who serves to give attention to serving, and the teacher to teach. Encouragement has its own work. Those who give should give generously, those who lead should do it diligently, and those who show mercy should do it cheerfully. The picture of a living sacrifice has become a picture of people doing the work their neighbours need.~Love must be sincere. Paul asks them to hate evil and hold to what is good, to care for each other with family affection and to take the lead in giving honour. They are to serve the Lord with zeal, rejoice in hope, endure trouble and keep praying. They should help meet the needs of fellow believers and make a habit of welcoming strangers. Rejoicing with those who rejoice and weeping with those who weep require attention to what is happening in another person's life. Paul asks them to live in harmony and associate with people of low position.~The harder instructions concern those who harm them. Bless people who persecute you, Paul says, and repay nobody evil for evil. As far as it depends on them, they are to live at peace with everyone. They must leave vengeance to God. Anyone whose enemy is hungry can give him food. Anyone whose enemy is thirsty can give him a drink. Paul ends with a choice his readers can carry into a quarrel or an injury. They can overcome evil with good.|The offering of the body becomes visible in the way people use gifts and respond to others. In Paul's examples, even hostility becomes an occasion to do good.|Which of Paul's instructions gives you a way to act towards someone with whom you are in conflict?
13:1-14|Duties to Authorities and to Neighbours|Paying what is owed and walking in the light|Paul has just asked his readers to live at peace with others as far as it depends on them. He now turns to the authorities under whom they live. He tells them to submit to governing authorities because authority has its place under God. Resisting that authority brings judgment. Paul describes the office in terms of the work it is meant to do. The ruler serves God for the good of the person who does right and bears the sword to punish wrongdoing. A person should therefore submit for conscience's sake as well as from concern about punishment.~The duties that follow are ordinary enough to recognise. Paul says this is why the readers pay taxes. The authorities are attending to their work as public servants. Each person should receive what is owed, whether taxes, customs duties, respect or honour. Paul moves directly from these obligations to a debt that remains whenever one person deals with another. They should keep owing one another love. The person who loves a neighbour has fulfilled the law.~Paul reminds them of commands against adultery, murder, stealing and coveting. Their common direction is expressed in the command to love one's neighbour as oneself. Love does no harm to a neighbour. That is how it fulfils the law. A reader can think about each command from the side of the person who would suffer if it were broken. The neighbour is the one whose marriage, life or possessions are treated with care when love governs what a person does.~Paul urges them to act with an awareness of the time. The hour has come to wake from sleep, for salvation is nearer now than when they first believed. The night is nearly over and the day is near. He asks them to cast away the works of darkness and put on the armour of light. They are to walk honourably as in the daytime, leaving behind drunken revelry, sexual immorality, quarrelling and jealousy. Paul closes by telling them to put on the Lord Jesus Christ and make no provision for the desires of the flesh. The approaching day gives urgency to the choices they can make while it is still dark.|Paul gives particular duties their place and then describes love as a debt that keeps making claims on us. 14:1-23|Different Tables Within One Fellowship|Making room for conscience|Paul asks the believers to welcome a person whose faith is weak without pulling him into quarrels about disputed matters. The person who eats must take care not to despise the one who abstains, and the one who abstains must take care not to judge the person who eats. God has welcomed him. Each is another master's servant. It is before his own master that he stands or falls, and the Lord is able to make him stand.~The same issue comes up over days. One person regards a day as special, while another regards all days alike. Paul asks each to be fully convinced in his own mind. The person who observes a day does it for the Lord. The person who eats gives thanks, and the one who abstains also gives thanks. They live and die to the Lord because they belong to him. Christ died and lived again to be Lord of the dead and the living. Each person will give an account to God.
