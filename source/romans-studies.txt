1:1-17|Paul Explains Why He Wants to Come to Rome|A gospel meant for every hearer|Paul introduces himself as set apart for the gospel God promised through the prophets. He gives thanks for the Roman believers' faith and tells them he prays to visit. He expects them to encourage one another. He says he owes the message to people of different backgrounds and that the gospel is God's power for salvation to all who believe.|Paul writes with a calling to teach and an expectation of receiving encouragement. The people he hopes to visit already have faith from which he expects to benefit.|What does Paul expect to give and receive if he visits Rome?
1:18-32|When Gratitude Gives Way to Idols|Honouring the Creator|Paul describes people who can see evidence of God's power in creation yet refuse to honour him or give thanks. Their thinking darkens and they exchange God's glory for images of created things. Paul traces how distorted worship affects conduct and relationships, including the way people approve in others what they practise themselves.|Paul begins the account with a refusal to honour and thank God. Gratitude helps keep the gifts of creation in relation to the one who gives them.|Which changes in conduct does Paul trace back to a change in worship?
2:1-16|The Person Who Judges Is Also Accountable|Receiving kindness as a call to repent|Paul turns towards the person who condemns another while doing similar things. He asks whether that person understands where God's patience and goodness are meant to lead. He describes God's impartial judgement and speaks of the law, conscience and hidden things that God will judge through Jesus Christ.|God's patience gives time to repent. It is easy to interpret that time as approval when we are occupied with pointing out another person's faults.|Where is Paul asking his reader to turn attention after condemning someone else?
2:17-29|The Teacher Under His Own Teaching|Integrity that reaches the heart|Paul addresses the person who relies on the law and claims to guide others. He asks whether the teacher also teaches himself. He describes how a public profession can be contradicted by conduct. He then speaks of the significance of circumcision in relation to obedience, ending with the inward work of the Spirit and praise from God.|The responsibility to teach gives the teacher an additional reason to examine his own life. Public recognition cannot substitute for God's judgement of the heart.|Which of Paul's questions would help you examine a duty you urge on other people?
3:1-20|Every Mouth Comes to Silence|Honesty before God|Paul asks what advantage Israel received and names the words of God entrusted to the Jews. Human unfaithfulness does not undo God's faithfulness. He then gathers testimony from Scripture about sin in speech, conduct and worship. His argument brings both Jew and Gentile under sin and leaves every mouth stopped before God.|A genuine privilege can make accountability deeper. The Scripture Paul cites gives people words for their condition before it gives them grounds for hope.|What does Paul say the law makes known when people stand accountable before God?
3:21-31|The Righteousness God Makes Known|Grace gives no room for boasting|Paul announces God's righteousness through faith in Jesus Christ. All have sinned, and justification is given freely by grace through the redemption in Christ. He speaks of God's justice and of God justifying those who believe. This leaves no ground for boasting. The one God justifies both Jew and Gentile through faith.|The same grace meets people with different histories. Boasting loses its ground when acceptance depends on what God gives through Christ.|How does the conclusion about boasting follow from the way Paul describes justification?
4:1-12|Abraham Before the Sign|Trust in the God who justifies|Paul asks when Abraham's faith was counted as righteousness. Scripture records it before circumcision. Paul compares wages owed to someone with righteousness counted through faith. He brings in David's words about the blessing of forgiven sin. Abraham receives circumcision as a sign and seal of what was already counted to him.|Paul pays attention to the timing of the sign. That sequence gives people beyond Abraham's physical descendants a place in the story of faith.|What happened before Abraham received the sign, and why is the order important to Paul?
4:13-25|The Promise Faced by an Old Man|Hope grounded in the power of God|Paul connects the promise to faith so that it can rest on grace. He recalls Abraham facing his own age and Sarah's inability to bear a child. Abraham glorifies God because he is persuaded God can do what he promised. Paul then connects that trust with believers who trust the God who raised Jesus from the dead.|Abraham's trust takes the facts of his condition seriously. Its ground is what God is able to do.|What two human limitations does Paul name when he describes Abraham's faith?
5:1-11|Peace After Justification|Hope in the midst of trouble|Paul says that through faith believers have peace with God and access to grace. He describes suffering producing endurance, character and hope, and speaks of God's love poured into hearts through the Holy Spirit. Christ died for people while they were still sinners, and Paul finds assurance in the reconciliation God has already accomplished.|Christian hope in this passage begins with reconciliation God has given. It makes room to speak honestly about suffering while trusting the love that met people in their weakness.|How does Christ's death for sinners support Paul's confidence about what lies ahead?
5:12-21|Adam and Christ|Grace that abounds over sin|Paul sets Adam's disobedience and its consequences beside the obedience of Jesus Christ. Death reigns through the trespass, while God's gift brings justification and life through Christ. Paul also says the law made the trespass increase. He closes by describing grace reigning through righteousness to eternal life.|Paul places humanity's condition inside a larger account of what Christ has done. The gift is described as greater than the ruin it answers.|What changes when Paul moves from what entered through Adam to what comes through Christ?
6:1-14|Buried and Raised With Christ|Living in the newness of life|Paul asks whether people should continue in sin because grace has increased. He turns to baptism into Christ's death and burial and to walking in newness of life. He calls believers to reckon themselves dead to sin and alive to God and to offer themselves and their bodies to God as instruments of righteousness.|Grace gives believers reason to change whom they obey. Paul asks them to offer their actual capacities to God in the life they now live.|What would presenting one part of your daily life as an instrument of righteousness involve?
6:15-23|The Master You Obey|Freedom that bears the fruit of holiness|Paul compares obedience to serving a master. He describes believers obeying from the heart and becoming servants of righteousness. He asks what fruit they gained from conduct they now regard with shame. Service to God bears fruit in holiness. Sin pays wages that end in death, while eternal life is God's gift through Christ.|Paul asks his hearers to compare where two kinds of obedience lead. Choices that look isolated can form habits that direct an entire life.|What fruit and conclusion does Paul associate with service to God?
7:1-13|The Command That Reveals Coveting|Recognising what the law brings to light|Paul uses marriage law to illustrate release from a former legal relation through death. Believers belong to the risen Christ to bear fruit for God and serve in newness of Spirit. He recalls how the command against coveting exposed sin, and how sin took occasion through the command. The law and command remain holy and good.|A good command can uncover a desire we had allowed to pass unnoticed. Paul distinguishes what the command shows from the sin that takes advantage of it.|Which command does Paul use to explain how he came to recognise coveting?
7:14-25|The Good I Want to Do|Taking inward conflict seriously|Paul gives voice to a painful conflict. The speaker approves the good and nevertheless sees another power at work in what he does. The cry for deliverance leads to thanksgiving to God through Jesus Christ. The passage describes the limits of wanting good when sin continues to assert itself.|Honest confession can name the gap between conviction and conduct. Paul's cry places hope of deliverance with God through Jesus Christ.|What tension is expressed between what the speaker approves and what he actually does?
8:1-17|The Spirit and the Cry of Adoption|Living as children who call on the Father|Paul announces freedom from condemnation in Christ. He describes the Spirit giving life and leading believers to put sinful deeds to death. Those led by the Spirit are God's children. They receive adoption and cry to the Father. Paul joins their inheritance with Christ to sharing in his suffering and glory.|The Spirit leads believers into a family relationship with obligations and assurance. They can address God as Father even in circumstances that carry a cost.|How does adoption change the way believers approach God according to Paul?
8:18-39|Creation Groans and Love Holds|Hope when words are difficult|Paul describes creation and believers groaning while they wait for redemption. The Spirit helps weakness and intercedes when people do not know what to pray. Paul speaks of God's purpose, Christ's intercession and the confidence that trouble, danger or death cannot separate God's people from his love in Christ.|Paul gives groaning and assurance space in the same argument. Weakness in prayer is met by the Spirit's help and by confidence in God's love.|Where does Paul direct a believer who is too weak to know how to pray?
9:1-29|Paul Grieves for Israel|Mercy and the faithfulness of God|Paul speaks of deep sorrow for his own people and recalls Israel's privileges. He asks how God's word stands when not all have responded, distinguishing physical descent from the children of promise. He recalls Isaac, Jacob, Moses and Pharaoh, then uses the image of a potter and cites the prophets about mercy and a remnant.|Paul's argument begins with grief for real people. His appeal to mercy calls for humility when considering God's freedom and the limits of human claims.|What privileges does Paul remember before asking how God's promise stands?
9:30-10:21|The Message Comes Near|Righteousness received by faith and proclaimed|Paul describes Gentiles receiving righteousness by faith while Israel stumbles in pursuing righteousness through its own efforts. He prays for Israel and speaks of Christ in relation to the law. The message is near: confession and faith concern Jesus as Lord and God's raising him. Paul then traces the need for people to be sent, preach, be heard and call on the Lord.|Paul's concern for his people becomes prayer and an argument for proclamation. The sequence makes the work of bringing the message to others a practical responsibility.|What links does Paul name between someone being sent and a hearer calling on the Lord?
11:1-24|Unexpected Branches on an Olive Tree|Humility after being welcomed|Paul asks whether God has cast away his people and points to himself and the remnant in Elijah's time. He describes some branches broken off and wild branches grafted into an olive tree. The grafted branches are warned against boasting because the root supports them. God is able to graft the former branches in again.|The image gives people included through faith a reason for gratitude and reverence. It also keeps open a possibility they may be tempted to dismiss in others.|How does Paul's reminder about the root answer the temptation to boast?
11:25-36|The Argument Ends in Praise|A place for humility before God's wisdom|Paul explains a mystery so Gentile believers will avoid conceit. He speaks of Israel, the fullness of the Gentiles and God's mercy, and describes God's gifts and calling as irrevocable. Rather than ending with confidence in his own understanding, he breaks into praise of the depth of God's wisdom and knowledge.|Paul has reasoned carefully and still ends in worship. The passage shows the place of humility after patient study of a difficult subject.|What reasons for humility does Paul give before he turns to praise?
12:1-21|A Living Sacrifice in Ordinary Relationships|Love that takes visible form|Paul appeals to God's mercies and asks believers to offer their bodies and be transformed by a renewed mind. He compares different gifts to members of one body. He then describes love in practice: honouring others, sharing with people in need, persevering in prayer, blessing persecutors and giving food or drink to an enemy.|The offering of the body becomes visible in the way people use gifts and respond to others. In Paul's examples, even hostility becomes an occasion to do good.|Which of Paul's instructions gives you a way to act towards someone with whom you are in conflict?
13:1-14|Duties to Authorities and to Neighbours|Paying what is owed and walking in the light|Paul discusses duties towards governing authorities and says to render taxes, respect and honour as due. He turns to the continuing duty of love, which seeks no harm to a neighbour and fulfils the law. Using the image of night passing and day drawing near, he urges his hearers to put on Christ.|Paul gives particular duties their place and then describes love as a debt that keeps making claims on us. The approaching day gives urgency to daily conduct.|How does Paul connect love of neighbour with the commands he names?
14:1-23|Different Tables Within One Fellowship|Making room for conscience|Some believers eat freely and others abstain. Some distinguish days, and others regard days alike. Paul tells them to receive one another and remember that each answers to the Lord. He asks people to consider whether their conduct causes another to stumble and to pursue peace and what builds up the fellowship.|Paul gives weight to both conscience and the effect of freedom on another person. A choice can be permitted and still be unhelpful in a particular company.|Why might a believer freely give up something at a shared meal?
15:1-13|The Strong Bear With the Weak|Welcome patterned on Christ|Paul asks the strong to bear with those who struggle and to seek their neighbours' good. He points to Christ's example and speaks of Scripture giving endurance and hope. He asks believers to receive one another as Christ received them. He then gathers Scriptures about the Gentiles sharing in praise.|Welcome is work someone has to do, especially where strengths and convictions differ. Paul gives it a common purpose in the glory of God.|How does Paul's instruction to the strong follow from what he says about Christ?
15:14-33|A Collection, a Journey and a Request for Prayer|Serving across distances|Paul explains his calling among the Gentiles. He hopes to visit Rome on his way to Spain, but first he is carrying a contribution from Macedonia and Achaia to poor believers in Jerusalem. He asks the Romans to join him in prayer for the journey and for the gift to be received.|Paul sees practical support as part of fellowship across communities. He also asks believers who may never travel with him to share in the work through prayer.|What work takes Paul to Jerusalem before his hoped-for visit to Rome?
16:1-27|The People Who Carried the Work|Recognising service by name|Paul commends Phoebe and asks the Romans to help her. He greets Priscilla and Aquila and many others who have worked, hosted churches or shared danger. He warns against people who cause division and sends greetings from his companions, including Tertius, who writes down the letter. The letter ends in praise to God.|Paul remembers people by name and sometimes tells what their work cost. The greetings put the earlier teaching about one body into the life of particular households and workers.|Which acts of service are made visible in the greetings Paul sends?
