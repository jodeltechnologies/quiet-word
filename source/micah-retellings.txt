1:1-3:12|The Houses Taken From Their Owners|Judgment begins where power has become a means of theft|Micah of Moresheth spoke in the days of Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah, and his vision concerned Samaria and Jerusalem. He called the peoples to hear as the Lord came forth as witness. Mountains melted and valleys split beneath him. Samaria's idols and the gains connected to them would be broken down, while the wound reached as far as the gate of Jerusalem. Micah mourned as the danger approached Judah. The names of towns in his lament carried words of grief, shame and loss, and the inhabitants were called to face the exile of children they delighted in. The judgment was heard in the places where people lived and raised their families.~Micah then led his hearers into the bedrooms of those who devised wrongdoing at night. They could carry it out in the morning because they had the power. Fields they coveted were seized, houses they wanted were taken, and people lost the inheritance on which their households depended. The Lord announced a calamity from which the offenders would be unable to free their necks. The reversal matched their conduct. People who measured and took the land of others would hear a lament over the dividing of their own land. Micah's charge shows that the ability to complete a transaction says little about whether acquiring the property was just.~Some wanted the preaching to stop. Micah asked whether the Lord's ways could be charged with impatience and whether his words did good to those who walked uprightly. Meanwhile, women were driven from pleasant homes and children lost what should have remained to them. The people preferred a speaker who would promise wine and strong drink. Into this account of dispossession came a promise that the Lord would gather the remnant like sheep in a fold. The one who broke open the way would lead them through the gate, their king going before them with the Lord at their head. Return would depend on the God whose warning they had tried to silence.~The leaders were expected to know justice, yet Micah pictured their treatment of the people as the tearing and cooking of flesh. The prophets declared peace when fed and announced war against those who gave them nothing. Night would come upon their visions. Micah described himself as filled with power by the Spirit of the Lord to declare Israel's sin. Rulers judged for reward, priests taught for wages and prophets divined for money, all while claiming that the Lord was among them and calamity could never come. Micah answered that Zion would be ploughed like a field. Their confidence in the holy city made their conduct more grievous.|God judges the use of influence to dispossess others and the use of religious authority to shield injustice.|Where might your ability to get your way keep you from seeing what another person stands to lose?
4:1-5:15|From the Ruined Hill to a Place of Peace|The remnant, the ruler from Bethlehem and the removal of false security|After announcing that Zion would be ploughed, Micah saw the mountain of the Lord's house established above the hills in days to come. Peoples would go there to learn God's ways, and his word would go out from Jerusalem. The Lord would judge between nations. Swords would be beaten into ploughshares and spears into pruning hooks. People would sit under their own vines and fig trees with no one to make them afraid. The vision reaches into the daily life of the household. A family could sit beneath what it had planted and enjoy its fruit because the violence that made such ordinary pleasures uncertain had ceased.~The promise then turned towards people who could scarcely imagine such security. The Lord would gather the lame, the driven away and those he had afflicted, and would reign over them in Zion. The daughter of Zion was told that she would go out from the city, dwell in the open country and reach Babylon. There the Lord would redeem her. Nations gathered against Zion expected to see her defiled, but they did not know the Lord's purpose in gathering them. Micah let the threat of exile and the promise of restoration stand close together. The coming distress would be real, and God's care would reach the people even in the place of their banishment.~A siege and the humiliation of Israel's judge give way to the promise of a ruler from Bethlehem Ephrathah, a place small among the clans of Judah. His origins are from ancient days. After a period of waiting, he would stand and shepherd his people in the strength and majesty of the Lord, and they would dwell securely. Micah says that he would be their peace. The promise speaks into the threat of Assyria and describes deliverance when the enemy enters the land. In the New Testament, the Bethlehem passage is recalled in connection with the birth of Jesus, whose coming brings renewed attention to Micah's image of a shepherd ruler.~The remnant of Jacob would be among many peoples like dew from the Lord and also like a lion among flocks. Micah then described what God would remove from his own people. Horses, chariots and fortified places would be cut off, along with sorceries, idols and sacred pillars. The people would cease bowing to the work of their hands. God would also judge the nations that had refused to obey. The close of the passage gives substance to the promise of peace. God's people would have to part with practices and objects in which they had placed their confidence. A restored community would learn to receive security from its shepherd.|God gathers scattered people and calls them to trust his rule with the security they once sought elsewhere.|What source of security would be hardest for you to surrender if it stood in the way of trusting God?
6:1-7:20|What the Lord Requires and What He Forgives|A covenant hearing, an honest confession and hope in God's mercy|Micah called the mountains and the foundations of the earth to hear the Lord's case against his people. God asked how he had burdened them and invited an answer. He reminded them of bringing them up from Egypt, redeeming them from slavery and sending Moses, Aaron and Miriam before them. They were to remember what Balak planned and what Balaam answered, and to recall the journey from Shittim to Gilgal. The hearing began with acts of deliverance. The people were being asked to consider their present conduct in the light of a history in which the Lord had repeatedly acted for their good.~The worshipper asked what to bring before the Lord. The questions moved from burnt offerings to thousands of rams, rivers of oil and finally a firstborn child. Micah answered with what God had already made known. The Lord required people to do justice, love mercy and walk humbly with him. The words were immediately tested against life in the city. Short measures, dishonest weights, violence and lying were present. People would eat and remain hungry, sow and fail to reap, tread olives and fail to anoint themselves with oil. The practices associated with Omri and Ahab had consequences that would reach the food, work and reputation of the community.~Micah lamented like someone looking for the first ripe fig after harvest and finding none. The faithful had disappeared from the land in his description. People lay in wait for blood, officials demanded gifts and judges took bribes. Even family relationships became unsafe. Micah turned his attention to the Lord and said he would wait for the God of his salvation. The voice that follows accepts responsibility for sin and expects to bear the Lord's indignation until he takes up the case and brings the sufferer into the light. The confession allows grief, responsibility and hope to remain in the same prayer. The enemy is warned against rejoicing at a fall that will give way to rising.~As Micah looked ahead to rebuilding and the gathering of people from afar, he asked the Lord to shepherd his people. God promised wonders like those of the exodus, and the nations would see and tremble. The final question echoes the prophet's name by asking who is a God like the Lord. He pardons iniquity and passes over the transgression of his remnant because he delights in mercy. He will tread sins underfoot and cast them into the depths of the sea, keeping faith with Jacob and showing the love pledged to Abraham. The book ends by putting the hope of restoration in the character of the God who heard the case.|The God who requires justice, mercy and humility delights in forgiving those who return to him.|What would doing justice and loving mercy look like in one transaction or relationship you will face this week?
