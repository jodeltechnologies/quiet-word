1:1-18|Hannah Pours Out Her Grief at Shiloh|Bringing sorrow honestly before God|Every year Elkanah travelled from his home in the hill country of Ephraim to worship and sacrifice at Shiloh. His household went with him. Peninnah had sons and daughters, while Hannah had no children. Elkanah loved Hannah and gave her a special portion when the family shared the sacrificial meal. Yet his affection could not take away the grief she carried. Peninnah repeatedly used Hannah's childlessness to hurt her. The journey to worship became another occasion for provocation. Year after year Hannah wept and lost her appetite. Elkanah asked why she was so sorrowful and whether his love meant more to her than ten sons. His questions show his concern, but Hannah still had something she needed to bring before God.~After the meal at Shiloh, Hannah rose and prayed. Eli the priest was sitting beside a doorway of the sanctuary. Hannah wept bitterly as she addressed the Lord of hosts. She asked Him to look on her distress, remember her and give her a son. With that request she made a vow. If God gave her the child, she would give him to the Lord for his whole life, and no razor would come upon his head. Her prayer joined a deeply personal longing to a promise of lifelong dedication. The account lets us hear her grief and her request without pretending that the years of waiting had been easy.~Hannah continued praying, and Eli watched her mouth. Her lips moved, but he heard no voice. He concluded that she was drunk and rebuked her, telling her to put away her wine. At the very place where she had come with her sorrow, Hannah now had to answer a mistaken accusation. She replied respectfully. She had drunk neither wine nor strong drink. She was a woman in deep distress, pouring out her soul before the Lord. Eli had seen the movement of her lips but had misunderstood the heart behind it. Hannah asked him to recognise the abundance of grief that had kept her praying there.~Eli changed his response. He told her to go in peace and asked that the God of Israel grant what she had requested. Hannah received his words and went away. She ate, and her face was no longer downcast. At this point in the story she still had no child, and the narrator has announced no pregnancy. What has changed is the woman who has brought her burden before the Lord. The household's painful circumstances have been told plainly, the priest's mistake has been corrected, and Hannah has left the sanctuary able to eat again. The next morning will bring another act of worship and the journey home.|Hannah brings her grief to God in honest prayer and answers a mistaken accusation with restraint. Her story invites us to pray sincerely and to listen carefully before judging another person's distress.|What do Hannah's prayer and her answer to Eli teach you about responding when sorrow is misunderstood?
1:19-2:11|The Child Hannah Brought Back to God|Keeping a promise after prayer is answered|Early the next morning Elkanah's household worshipped before the Lord and returned home to Ramah. In time Hannah conceived and gave birth to a son. She called him Samuel, connecting his name with the request she had made to the Lord. The account says that the Lord remembered her. The child whose absence had been the occasion of such grief now lay in her care. When Elkanah prepared for the next yearly journey to offer sacrifice and fulfil his vow, Hannah stayed home. She explained that she would bring Samuel after he was weaned, so that he could appear before the Lord and remain there. Elkanah agreed that she should care for him until that time.~When Samuel was weaned, Hannah took him to Shiloh with offerings of animals, flour and wine. He was still young. The family offered a sacrifice and brought the child to Eli. Hannah reminded the priest who she was. She was the woman who had stood there beside him praying. This was the child for whom she had asked, and the Lord had granted her petition. She now gave him to the Lord for the whole of his life. The promise made in sorrow became an act of obedience after joy had come. The narrator records worship there, placing the child's arrival at Shiloh within the family's response to God.~Hannah's prayer then rises beyond her own household. Her heart rejoices in the Lord and in His salvation. She declares that no one is holy like Him and that there is no rock like their God. Proud speech has no secure foundation before the God who knows and weighs human actions. She describes mighty warriors losing strength while those who stumbled are made strong. The well fed seek work for bread, while the hungry cease to hunger. Her words about the barren woman and the mother of many belong to a larger picture of God overturning circumstances that people assume will last. The prayer's picture of a barren woman bearing seven expresses fullness in its song of reversal. Samuel remains the one child whose birth has been narrated here.~Hannah goes on to speak of the Lord bringing down and raising up, making poor and making rich. He lifts the needy from the dust and gives them a place of honour. He guards His faithful people, judges His enemies and gives strength to His king and His anointed. Her prayer looks beyond her present joy toward God's rule over the earth. Then the family returns to Ramah. Samuel stays behind and begins to minister before the Lord under Eli. Hannah has asked, received, returned and worshipped. Samuel's life of service begins where his mother once stood in tears.|Answered prayer calls for gratitude and faithfulness. Hannah keeps her vow and praises the God whose power reaches beyond her own changed circumstances.|How does Hannah's prayer move from her personal answer to the character and rule of God?
2:12-36|The Priests Who Took What They Wanted|Responsibility in the house of God|Samuel's service at Shiloh takes place among men who misuse their sacred responsibility. Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, serve as priests, yet the account says that they do not know the Lord. When worshippers prepare the sacrificial meat, a servant comes with a three pronged fork and takes whatever it brings out of the pot for the priest. Sometimes the servant demands raw meat before the fat has been offered to God. If a worshipper asks that God's portion be offered first, the servant threatens to take the meat by force. The priests' appetite has become an excuse to intimidate the people bringing sacrifices. Their sin is very great before the Lord, and people come to despise His offering.~Alongside that conduct, the narrator shows Samuel serving as a child in a linen ephod. Hannah makes him a little coat and brings it each year when she comes with Elkanah to offer sacrifice. The small yearly gift connects his new life of service with the mother who brought him there. Eli blesses his parents and asks that the Lord give Hannah children in place of the child she has entrusted to His service. Hannah later bears three sons and two daughters. Samuel continues growing before the Lord. His growth and his mother's visits unfold while the older priests persist in conduct that harms the worshippers.~Eli, now very old, hears what his sons are doing. He also hears that they are sleeping with the women who assemble at the entrance of the tabernacle. He challenges them about the reports and tells them they are causing the Lord's people to transgress. He warns of the seriousness of sinning against the Lord. They refuse to listen. The account connects their refusal with the judgment God intends to bring upon them. Meanwhile Samuel grows in favour with God and with people. The repeated notice of his growth places a faithful young servant within a household whose leaders are refusing correction.~A man of God comes to Eli with a message. He recalls the privilege God gave Eli's ancestral house, choosing it for priestly service and providing offerings for its support. Why, then, has Eli honoured his sons above God and shared in the benefit of their greed? Judgment will fall upon his household. Its strength will be cut off, its descendants will suffer loss, and Hophni and Phinehas will die on the same day as a sign. God will raise up a faithful priest who acts according to His heart. The passage ends with descendants of Eli's house reduced to asking for food and a place of service. Sacred office has brought responsibility, and God has seen what its holders chose to do.|Spiritual responsibility requires reverence, integrity and action against known wrongdoing. Eli's spoken warnings do not remove his accountability for honouring his sons above God.|What does God's message to Eli reveal about the difference between noticing wrongdoing and taking responsibility for it?
3:1-21|The Night Samuel Learned Who Was Calling|Hearing and faithfully delivering God's word|Samuel continues serving the Lord under Eli at a time when the word of the Lord is rare and visions are uncommon. Eli's sight has grown weak. One night he is lying in his place, and Samuel is also lying down in the sanctuary where the ark of God is kept. The lamp of God has not yet gone out. Into that ordinary night comes a call. The Lord calls Samuel, and the boy answers. Assuming Eli has summoned him, Samuel runs to the old priest and announces that he has come. Eli says he did not call and sends him back to bed. Samuel obeys, with no understanding yet of who has spoken.~The call comes again. Samuel gets up and returns to Eli, still certain that the voice belongs to the man under whom he serves. Eli again tells him to lie down. The narrator explains that Samuel does not yet know the Lord in this way, because the word of the Lord has not yet been revealed to him. A third call brings him to Eli once more. This time Eli understands that the Lord is calling the child. He gives Samuel an answer to make if the voice comes again. Samuel is to say that the Lord should speak, because His servant is listening. He goes back to his place. When the Lord comes and calls his name again, Samuel gives the answer Eli has taught him.~The message is severe. God is about to do something in Israel that will astonish those who hear it. Everything already spoken against Eli's household will be carried out. Eli knows the evil of his sons, and he has failed to restrain them. God declares that the iniquity of the house will not be dealt with by sacrifice or offering. Samuel's first recorded reception of God's word places a burden on him concerning the priest who has cared for him. The account gives no invented conversation to soften the message. Samuel lies until morning, then opens the doors of the Lord's house. He is afraid to tell Eli what he has seen and heard.~Eli summons him and insists that he withhold nothing. Samuel tells him everything. Eli acknowledges that it is the Lord and submits to what He judges good. From that difficult beginning, Samuel grows with the Lord's presence upon his life. God lets none of his words fall to the ground. Across Israel, from Dan to Beersheba, people recognise that Samuel has been established as the Lord's prophet. God continues appearing at Shiloh and revealing Himself to Samuel through His word. The child who first mistook the call for Eli's voice has become a recognised bearer of a message he must deliver faithfully, even when it concerns someone close to him.|A willingness to hear God includes a willingness to receive correction and speak truth faithfully. Samuel's fear does not prevent him from telling Eli the whole message.|Why was telling Eli the whole message a necessary part of Samuel's response to God's call?
4:1-22|The Ark Is Taken and Shiloh Hears the News|Reverence beyond religious confidence|Israel went out to fight the Philistines, camping near Ebenezer while the enemy gathered at Aphek. The first battle ended in defeat, with about four thousand Israelites killed. Back in camp the elders asked why the Lord had allowed them to be struck down. Their proposed answer was to fetch the ark of the covenant from Shiloh. They expected its arrival to save them from their enemies. The ark was brought into the camp accompanied by Hophni and Phinehas, the very priests whose wrongdoing had brought warnings of judgment upon Eli's house. When it arrived, Israel shouted so loudly that the ground seemed to ring with the sound.~The Philistines heard the shouting and learned that the ark had come. They were frightened. Their own words about Israel's God recalled the defeat of Egypt, though their description confused the details and spoke of mighty gods. They urged one another to fight courageously rather than become servants of the Hebrews. The next battle brought an even greater disaster for Israel. Thirty thousand foot soldiers fell, the survivors fled to their homes, and the Philistines captured the ark. Hophni and Phinehas both died. The sign announced earlier against Eli's family had come to pass. The cheering in Israel's camp had given way to flight, death and the loss of the sacred object in which the elders had placed such confidence.~A man from Benjamin escaped from the army and reached Shiloh that same day. His torn clothes and the earth upon his head showed that he carried news of disaster. Eli, ninety eight years old and nearly blind, sat watching beside the road, trembling with concern for the ark. The messenger's report set the city crying aloud. Eli heard the uproar and asked what had happened. The man told him of Israel's flight, the slaughter of the army and the deaths of his two sons. Last came the news that the ark had been captured. At that, Eli fell backward from his seat beside the gate. His neck broke, and he died. His forty years of judging Israel ended with the catastrophe he had been warned would fall upon his house.~Phinehas's wife was close to giving birth when she heard that the ark was taken and that her husband and father in law were dead. Labour came upon her, and she gave birth to a son. The women attending her tried to comfort her with the news of his birth, but she gave no answering attention. As she died, she named him Ichabod, connecting the name with the departure of glory from Israel. The loss of the ark dominates her final words. A child has been born into a household shattered by death, and Shiloh's grief brings the story of Eli's unrestrained sons to its dreadful outcome.|Religious symbols and confident words cannot take the place of reverence and obedience. Read the elders' decision alongside the earlier warnings to Eli's household, and examine where you may be relying on outward religion while resisting God's correction.|What did Israel expect the ark's arrival to accomplish, and what had the earlier chapters already revealed about the nation's spiritual condition?
5:1-12|Dagon Falls Before the Captured Ark|God's honour in the land of the enemy|The Philistines carried the ark from the battlefield to Ashdod and placed it in the temple of Dagon, beside the image of their god. They had won the battle and taken Israel's most sacred object. Inside Dagon's house, the ark stood in a setting that might have appeared to celebrate that victory. Yet the next morning the people of Ashdod found Dagon lying face down before the ark of the Lord. They lifted the image and put it back in its place. The account describes no Israelite army entering the temple and no priest of Israel acting to bring the idol down. The people who served Dagon had to raise him themselves.~The following morning they found the image fallen again. This time Dagon's head and both hands were broken off upon the threshold, leaving only his trunk. The narrator connects that event with the later practice of Dagon's priests and worshippers avoiding the threshold when they entered his temple. The damage to the image became part of the place's remembered history. Israel's defeat had not made the Lord powerless in Philistine territory. The ark had been captured, but what happened beside it overturned the apparent meaning of its presence in the temple. The image set above the worshippers could neither remain upright nor preserve its own head and hands.~The people of Ashdod also suffered beneath the heavy hand of the Lord. Destruction and painful swellings afflicted the city and its surrounding district. They recognised that the presence of the ark had become a danger to them and to Dagon. Gathering the Philistine rulers, they asked what should be done. The decision was to move the ark to Gath. There, too, disaster followed. People of every standing were struck, and great fear spread through the city. Sending the ark elsewhere had carried the crisis to another community. The narrative moves from the damaged idol to the suffering cities, keeping the Lord's power in view throughout.~The next destination was Ekron. Its people cried out when the ark arrived, protesting that it had been brought there to kill them and their people. Again the rulers assembled. The demand was now to send the ark back to its own place. Death had spread through the city, and those who survived were afflicted with swellings. The chapter closes with their cry reaching heaven. The Philistines had begun by bringing a captured object into a temple. They now sought relief from the God whose ark they possessed. Their discussions would soon turn to how they could return it and acknowledge the judgment that had followed its journey through their land.|God's power and honour do not depend upon human victory or defeat. The fallen image and the suffering cities call for humility before the Lord and a willingness to acknowledge His authority.|How does the movement from Dagon's temple to the Philistine cities change the meaning of the ark's capture?
6:1-7:1|The Cows Take the Road Back to Israel|Receiving God's presence with reverence|After seven months with the ark in their country, the Philistines summoned priests and diviners to advise them. They wanted to know how to send it home. The advisers called for a guilt offering, consisting of five golden images of the swellings and five golden mice, corresponding to the five Philistine rulers and representing the affliction upon their people and land. They urged the rulers to give glory to Israel's God and recalled Pharaoh's stubbornness. The memory of Egypt now became a warning against continuing to harden their own hearts. They proposed a test to discover whether the disasters had truly come from the Lord.~A new cart was prepared. Two cows that were nursing calves and had never worn a yoke were harnessed to it, while their calves were shut up at home. The ark was placed on the cart with a box containing the golden offerings beside it. The advisers said to watch its direction. If the cows went toward Beth shemesh, the Philistines would recognise that Israel's God had brought the disaster. If they went elsewhere, they would regard it as chance. The cows went straight along the road to Beth shemesh, lowing as they travelled and turning neither right nor left. The Philistine rulers followed as far as the border. The test they had chosen pointed to the answer they had feared.~At Beth shemesh, people were harvesting wheat in the valley. Looking up, they saw the ark and rejoiced. The cart stopped beside a large stone in the field of a man named Joshua. The Levites took down the ark and the box of golden objects and set them upon the stone. The people split the wood of the cart and offered the cows as a burnt offering, together with other sacrifices that day. The five Philistine rulers saw what happened and returned to Ekron. The account records the cities represented by the golden offerings and recalls the great stone where the ark had been set down. Its return had been witnessed on both sides of the border.~Joy at Beth shemesh was followed by mourning. The Lord struck people because they had looked into the ark, and the community lamented the great loss. They asked who could stand before the holy Lord God and where the ark should go next. Messengers went to Kiriath jearim, asking its inhabitants to fetch it. Those men came and brought it to Abinadab's house on the hill. They set apart his son Eleazar to keep it. The ark's journey had exposed both Philistine helplessness and Israel's need for reverence. Its return brought responsibility to the people who received it, and the story now turns toward the nation's relationship with the Lord.|Receiving what belongs to God calls for reverence as well as joy. The ark's return invites the people to honour the holy God who has made His power known among both nations.|Why must the rejoicing at Beth shemesh and the judgment that follows be read together?
7:2-17|The Stone Samuel Raised After Mizpah|Repentance and remembered help|The ark remained at Kiriath jearim, and a long period passed. Twenty years went by, and the people of Israel lamented after the Lord. Samuel addressed the nation with a clear call. If they were returning to the Lord with all their hearts, they must put away their foreign gods and the Ashtaroth, direct their hearts toward Him and serve Him alone. He promised that the Lord would deliver them from the Philistines. Israel responded by removing the Baals and Ashtaroth and serving the Lord only. Their grief had to become a change in whom they worshipped. Samuel then called them to gather at Mizpah, where he would pray for them.~The people assembled, drew water and poured it out before the Lord. They fasted and confessed that they had sinned against Him. Samuel judged Israel there. The gathering soon drew the attention of the Philistine rulers, who advanced against them. Israel heard of the approach and became afraid. This time their appeal was to Samuel to keep crying to the Lord for them, so that He would save them from their enemies. Earlier, the elders had brought the ark into battle and expected its presence to secure victory. Here the people had renounced their idols, confessed their sin and asked for prayer while facing the same powerful enemy.~Samuel took a young lamb and offered it wholly as a burnt offering. He cried to the Lord on Israel's behalf, and the Lord heard him. As the sacrifice was being offered and the Philistines drew near, the Lord thundered with a mighty sound against them. Their forces fell into confusion and were defeated before Israel. The Israelites went out from Mizpah and pursued them as far as below Beth car. Samuel then took a stone and set it between Mizpah and Shen. He called it Ebenezer and explained that the Lord had helped them thus far. The stone gave a visible place to the memory of help they had received.~The account describes the Philistines as subdued, with the Lord's hand against them throughout Samuel's days. Cities and surrounding territory previously taken by the Philistines were restored, and there was peace between Israel and the Amorites. Samuel continued judging Israel throughout his life. Each year he travelled a circuit through Bethel, Gilgal and Mizpah, administering justice in those places. He returned to Ramah, where he lived and also judged the people, and he built an altar to the Lord there. The story moves from the national crisis to the steady work that followed. Prayer, worship and justice belonged to Israel's continuing life after the battle, while the stone stood as a reminder of the One who had helped them.|Returning to God involves turning away from rival loyalties, confessing sin and depending upon His help. Remembering answered prayer should lead into continued worship and faithful conduct.|What actions show that Israel's return to the Lord at Mizpah went beyond feeling sorrow, and how can you practise that kind of repentance?
