Obed-edom's large family is associated with God's blessing, and its members are described as able and strong. Hosah had appointed Simri as chief although he was not the firstborn, a small detail showing that the record attends to actual household arrangements. These men were not incidental attendants. Guarding entrances to God's house required dependable people, and the account gives their families and abilities the same careful notice already given to other kinds of service.~Lots assigned the gates to small and great alike. Shelemiah received the east, his son Zechariah the north, Obed-edom the south and a responsibility for his sons in the storehouse area. Other men received the western approaches. Numbers are supplied for the daily stations, including the gates, the causeway and the area called Parbar. Zechariah is remembered as a wise counsellor, so the record values judgment along with physical strength. The arrangement made care for the house specific. Entrances and approaches were not left to whoever happened to be available. Named people knew the place they were to guard, and the shared pattern supported continuing watchfulness.~The chapter then turns to the treasures of God's house and the dedicated gifts. Levite families held these responsibilities, including descendants connected with Moses through Gershom and Eliezer. Shelomith and his brothers cared for offerings dedicated by David, family leaders and military commanders. Some came from spoils of war and were set aside to maintain God's house. The account also remembers gifts dedicated by Samuel, Saul, Abner and Joab. These objects remained entrusted property, whatever the wider histories of the people who had given them. Hebronite families handled responsibilities west of the Jordan, while men found at Jazer in Gilead were appointed over Reuben, Gad and the half tribe of Manasseh in matters concerning God and the king. The chapter thus reaches from a guarded doorway to distant tribal administration. Worship, money, judgment and public responsibility each required trustworthy service. Most of these men receive no dramatic personal story, yet their work helped sustain the life of the people. 27:1-34|The Kingdom Depends on Many Kinds of Work|Armies, farms and counsel all require responsible people|The preparations described in Chronicles extend into the administration of David's kingdom. Military divisions entered and left royal service month by month through the year. Each division numbered twenty-four thousand and had its appointed commander. Jashobeam led the first, Dodai the second and Benaiah the third. The remaining months receive their leaders in turn. Some names are familiar from the accounts of David's mighty men, including Sibbecai and Maharai. Asahel is named in connection with the fourth division, followed by his son Zebadiah. The list preserves an established pattern of service without requiring every man named to have held his place at precisely the same moment throughout the reign.~Leaders over the tribes are then recorded, including responsibilities among the Levites and Aaron's descendants. The account also returns briefly to the census. David had not counted those twenty years old and younger, remembering God's promise to multiply Israel like the stars. Joab had begun the numbering but had not completed it because wrath fell upon Israel, and that number was not entered in the account of David's chronicles. This reminder stands within a chapter full of legitimate organisation and numbers. The earlier census cannot therefore be dismissed as a harmless piece of administration. The writer keeps its guilt visible even while recording the orderly structures of the kingdom.~The next appointments concern the king's property and its produce. Azmaveth had charge of the royal treasures, while Jehonathan oversaw storehouses in fields, towns, villages and fortresses. Ezri supervised cultivation. Others cared for vineyards, wine supplies, olive and sycamore trees, and oil stores. Herds in Sharon and the valleys had their own overseers. Obil the Ishmaelite cared for camels, Jehdeiah for donkeys and Jaziz the Hagerite for flocks. These details bring the kingdom down to fields, animals and stored food. Royal life could not continue on military courage alone. It relied on land being worked, produce being kept and animals being tended by people whose names seldom appear in accounts of battle.~Finally, counsellors and close attendants are named. Jonathan is described as wise and as a scribe. Jehiel attended the king's sons. Ahithophel and his successors appear in the counsel given to the king, while Hushai is remembered as the king's companion and Joab as army commander. The chapter gathers a wide range of work without pretending that every servant performed the same task. Its order depends on distinct responsibilities carried out together. The reader sees an administration held up by courage, knowledge, agriculture, storage and personal counsel. Such a list gives dignity to practical competence. It also raises the question of whether influence and resources are being handled faithfully, since a large system can suffer greatly when the people within it neglect the part placed in their hands.|A community needs more than its most visible work. 28:1-21|David Gives Solomon the Plans and a Charge to Know God|The building must begin with a willing heart|David gathered Israel's leaders in Jerusalem. Tribal heads, military commanders, stewards of property, officers and valiant men were present. The aged king stood and addressed them as his brothers and his people. He explained his desire to build a resting place for the ark and the preparations he had made. He also repeated God's refusal: he had been a man of war and had shed blood. His own election as king remained a gift, from God's choice of Judah and Jesse's household to His choice of David among his brothers. Now Solomon had been chosen from David's many sons to occupy the throne and build God's house.~David placed obedience before the assembly. They were to keep and seek all God's commands so that they could possess the good land and leave it to their children. Then he addressed Solomon directly. He was to know his father's God and serve Him with an undivided heart and willing mind, because the Lord searches every heart and understands the thoughts within it. Seeking God carried a promise of finding Him, while forsaking Him carried a grave warning. The temple commission did not excuse Solomon from personal faithfulness. The son chosen for an extraordinary public work still needed to know and serve the God for whom the house would be built.~David handed over the plans. They included the porch, buildings, treasuries, upper rooms, inner rooms and the place of the mercy seat. He supplied arrangements for the courts, surrounding rooms, priestly and Levite divisions, and the vessels needed for service. Weights of gold and silver were set out for lampstands, tables, utensils, bowls and other objects. Refined gold was provided for the incense altar and for the cherubim spreading their wings above the ark. David said that the Lord had given him understanding of the design in writing. The record presents a prepared responsibility being passed from father to son, with detail sufficient to guide work beyond the moment of public encouragement.~David again told Solomon to be strong, courageous and active. He was not to give way to fear or dismay, because the Lord would be with him and would not abandon him before the work was finished. Priests, Levites, willing skilled workers, leaders and people were available to assist. Solomon did not receive the charge as an isolated person expected to invent every detail and carry every burden. He received plans, resources, helpers and the assurance of God's presence. Yet all these supports followed the deeper appeal to know God and serve Him willingly. The building's splendour could never replace that relationship. David's final encouragement joined the heart of the worker to the actual work, calling Solomon to rise and do what God had entrusted to him.|Preparation and help are valuable, but public service must rest on personally knowing and obeying God. 29:1-30|The People Give Gladly and David's Reign Ends|Everything offered to God has first come from Him|David reminded the assembly that Solomon was young and that the work was great because the house was for the Lord God. He described the materials already prepared with all his strength: precious metals, iron, wood and stones of many kinds. Then he added a personal gift from his own possessions because his affection was set on God's house. Three thousand talents of gold and seven thousand talents of refined silver were offered beyond the earlier provision. David asked who else would willingly dedicate themselves to the Lord that day. Leaders, commanders and overseers responded with substantial gifts of metals and precious stones. The people rejoiced because the offerings were willing and wholehearted, and David shared their joy.~Before the whole assembly, David blessed God. Greatness, power, glory, victory and majesty belonged to the Lord, together with everything in heaven and earth. Riches and honour came from Him, and the strength to become great rested in His hand. David then asked who he and his people were that they could give so willingly. All things had come from God, and what they offered was already His. Their days were passing like a shadow, as those of their ancestors had passed. The enormous provision did not make its donors self-sufficient. David's prayer placed their temporary lives and borrowed possessions beneath the enduring rule of the One they worshipped.~He acknowledged that God tests the heart and delights in uprightness. He prayed that the willing spirit seen that day would remain in the people's thoughts and that their hearts would be directed towards God. He also asked for Solomon to receive a wholehearted desire to keep God's commands and complete the house. At David's call the assembly blessed the Lord, bowed and paid their reverence. Sacrifices followed in abundance, and they ate and drank before God with great gladness. Solomon was made king a second time in this public account and anointed for rule, while Zadok was anointed as priest. The leaders, mighty men and David's sons submitted to Solomon, and God gave him great majesty before Israel.~The book ends by looking back over David's reign. He had ruled forty years, seven in Hebron and thirty-three in Jerusalem. He died at a good old age, full of days, riches and honour, and Solomon succeeded him. The closing notice points to records associated with Samuel, Nathan and Gad, covering David's deeds and the times through which he and Israel had passed. The story that began with Adam and moved through families now leaves a prepared temple project and an established successor. David would not see the building finished. His last great act of preparation belonged to a future carried by others. His prayer supplies the right measure for all the wealth, work and honour described: everything received had come from God, and everything offered was being returned to Him.|Generosity grows through recognising God's ownership.
