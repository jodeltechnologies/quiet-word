1:1-27|David Receives the Crown and Sings a Lament|Grieving without celebrating an enemy's fall|David had returned to Ziklag after defeating the Amalekite raiders. On the third day a man arrived from Israel's camp with torn clothes and earth upon his head. He bowed before David and reported the army's flight and the deaths of Saul and Jonathan. David asked how he knew. The messenger described finding Saul on Mount Gilboa, pursued by enemy forces, and claimed that Saul had asked him to end his life. He said he had done so and brought the crown and bracelet from the king's body. This is the messenger's account of his own action. The closing chapter of 1 Samuel has already described Saul falling upon his sword. The narrator now shows what David does with the claim made before him.~David and his men tore their clothes. They mourned, wept and fasted until evening for Saul, Jonathan and the fallen people of Israel. The death of the king who had hunted him was received as a national sorrow. David questioned the messenger further and learned that he was an Amalekite resident among Israel. He asked why the man had not feared to strike the Lord's anointed. Calling an attendant, David ordered his execution. He held the man accountable by his own testimony that he had killed Saul. The crown delivered into David's hands brought no reward to the bearer. David continued the refusal to treat the destruction of Saul as a service he wished others to perform.~He then composed a lament over Saul and Jonathan. The account connects its preservation with the Book of Jashar and its teaching among the people of Judah. David mourned Israel's fallen splendour and did not want Philistine towns to celebrate the news. His words turned toward Gilboa, where shields lay dishonoured, and remembered the courage of Saul and Jonathan in battle. He spoke of Jonathan's bow and Saul's sword, their strength and swiftness, and the unity of their deaths. He called the women of Israel to weep for the king under whom they had received prosperity. The lament remembers what the nation's loss includes, even after all that Saul's later conduct had done to David.~The song becomes deeply personal as David addresses Jonathan. His friend had been dear to him, and the love shown in their covenant friendship had been extraordinary. The repeated cry over the fallen mighty men holds public grief and personal bereavement together. David offers no triumphant announcement of his own future in the poem. The book opens with a crown brought to Ziklag, but the first sustained response is mourning. Israel has lost its king, David has lost his faithful friend, and the weapons of its warriors lie useless. The question of who will rule next follows this act of remembrance, rather than erasing it.|Another person's downfall can bring relief and still call for grief. He is Jesse's son, the man raised to a high place, the anointed of Jacob's God and Israel's beloved singer. Those titles hold his family origin, public responsibility and worship together. He speaks of the Spirit of the Lord speaking through him and of God's word upon his tongue. The declaration claims its authority from the God of Israel, called the Rock of Israel. At the centre stands an instruction concerning rule over human beings. The ruler must be just and govern in the fear of God. The closing account of David's reign therefore includes a standard before it turns to the names and exploits of his warriors.~Just rule is compared with morning sunlight, a clear dawn and the fresh growth that follows rain. The picture makes the effects of authority visible through the life it encourages. People under a ruler need justice as growing things need light and water. The image also asks the reader to consider the record of the kingdom already told. David has administered justice, but he has also used power to take, conceal and destroy. The requirement that the ruler fear God applies to the anointed speaker himself. His office does not place him above the standard he announces. In the wording of the King James passage, the condition of his house does not provide a simple match for the ideal just described. Yet the covenant is ordered and secure, and David speaks of it as the ground of his salvation and desire. His hope rests upon the commitment of God. That hope belongs beside the promised discipline and steadfast love recorded in chapter seven. The final words do not offer a new account of how every family failure will be repaired. They locate the future of his house within a promise whose faithfulness exceeds his own record.~The last image concerns the wicked, likened to thorns thrown aside. They cannot be grasped carelessly by an unprotected hand; iron and a spear shaft are required, and they are destined for burning. The passage therefore holds accountable leadership, enduring promise and judgment within a short poetic declaration. It does not yet narrate David's death, which belongs to the following biblical book. Here it supplies words by which his reign can be considered as its closing material unfolds.
