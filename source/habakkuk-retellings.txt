1:1-2:20|The Prophet Who Stayed at His Watch|Habakkuk brings his questions before God and learns how the righteous are to wait|Habakkuk sees violence around him and asks the Lord how long he must cry for help. Wrongdoing is in plain sight, quarrels multiply, and the law has lost its force. The wicked surround the righteous so that justice comes out twisted. The Lord directs him to look among the nations. He is raising up the Chaldeans, who sweep across the earth and seize homes that belong to others. They are dreaded and make their own rules. Their horsemen come from far away, swift as leopards and fierce as wolves at evening. They gather captives like sand, laugh at kings and pile up earth to take fortresses. In their forward rush they make a god of their own strength. The reply shows Habakkuk that God has heard his complaint. It also brings him face to face with a fresh difficulty.~Habakkuk turns again to the Lord, whom he knows as the Holy One from everlasting. He understands that God has appointed the invader for judgment and correction. Yet he asks how the Lord, whose eyes are pure, can look on while a treacherous people swallows those more righteous than itself. He pictures the nations as fish with nobody to rule them. The conqueror hauls them in with a hook and net, then sacrifices to the net because it has made him rich. Will he keep emptying it and killing nations without pity? Habakkuk stations himself at his watch to see how the Lord will answer. He has put his question plainly and now takes the place of someone ready to listen and be corrected.~The Lord tells him to write the vision clearly on tablets. It has an appointed time and will speak in the end. Even if it seems slow, Habakkuk is to wait for it. The proud person's soul is crooked, but the righteous shall live by faith. The invader, like someone made restless by wine, gathers nations with an appetite as wide as the grave. The peoples he gathered will take up a song against him. They pronounce woe on the man who accumulates what is someone else's, for the plundered nations will rise against him. They denounce the man who secures his house through evil gain. The stones and timber of the house will themselves testify to what he did.~The song speaks against building a town with blood and exhausting whole peoples for ends that come to nothing. The earth will be filled with the knowledge of the Lord's glory as the waters cover the sea. The one who makes his neighbours drunk in order to gaze upon their shame must drink the Lord's cup himself. His violence against Lebanon, its creatures and the people of the land comes back upon him. A final woe falls on those who ask wood and stone to wake and give them guidance. However richly an image is covered, it has no breath. The last words turn Habakkuk and his listeners towards the Lord in his holy temple. The whole earth is called to be silent before him. The prophet has heard that the conqueror himself will answer for the cruelty that seemed to go unchecked.|Faith can bring an honest complaint to God, listen for correction and obey while his appointed time is still ahead.|What unanswered question can you bring before God while continuing to do what you already know is right?
3:1-19|Joy When the Fields Give Nothing|A frightened prophet remembers God's works and learns to rejoice before relief arrives|Habakkuk's final chapter is a prayer meant to be sung. He has heard what the Lord has declared and is afraid. He asks the Lord to revive his work in the midst of the years, make it known and remember mercy in wrath. He then sees God coming from Teman and the Holy One from Mount Paran. His glory covers the heavens and the earth is full of his praise. Light shines from his hand, where his power is hidden. Pestilence goes before him. He stands and measures the earth, and nations are startled at his look. Mountains that seem to have stood forever scatter before him. The tents of Cushan and the curtains of Midian tremble. Habakkuk's prayer has grown from his immediate fear into a vision of the Lord before whom the lands and their inhabitants shake.~The prayer asks whether the Lord's anger was against rivers and sea when he rode upon his horses and chariots of salvation. His bow is uncovered, the earth is cleft by rivers, the mountains writhe, and the deep lifts its voice. Sun and moon stand still at the light of his arrows and spear. The Lord marches through the land in indignation and threshes the nations. Habakkuk then says why the Lord goes out. He goes for the salvation of his people and of his anointed. He strikes the head of the house of the wicked and lays its foundations bare. The warriors who came storming in to scatter the needy meet his power, and the prayer sees him treading the sea with his horses. The river and sea imagery belongs to a prayer about a people who need rescue and a God able to bring it.~Habakkuk now describes what hearing this has done to him. His body trembles, his lips quiver and weakness enters his bones. He waits quietly for the day of trouble to come upon the people who invade. The words that follow are spoken by a man who can still feel the fear in his body. He names the losses that could come upon the land. The fig tree may have no blossoms, the vines no fruit and the olive crop may fail. Fields may yield no food. The fold may be empty and no herd may stand in the stalls. Each loss takes something particular from a household, until Habakkuk has imagined the failure of nearly everything his neighbours depend upon.~Even with those losses before him, he says he will rejoice in the Lord and joy in the God of his salvation. The Lord God is his strength and gives him feet like a deer, able to walk on high places. The prayer closes with a direction to the chief musician for stringed instruments. Habakkuk gives the congregation words it can sing in the same uncertainty he has known. He began the book asking why God seemed slow to act. He ends it still waiting and able to name the one who will uphold him while he waits.|God remains the strength of his people through fear, scarcity and delay. Remembering his saving works gives us words to pray when present circumstances give us little comfort.|If a source of security were taken away this week, what past mercy could you remember when you pray?
