1:1-7:38|What Israel Brought to the Altar|The Lord teaches a redeemed people how to approach him with worship, confession and restitution|The Lord calls to Moses from the tent of meeting. A person bringing a burnt offering from the herd presents a male without blemish, lays a hand on its head and kills it before the Lord. Aaron's sons handle the blood and burn the prepared offering on the altar. Instructions also provide for offerings from the flock or of birds, so bringing an animal from the herd is one way of approaching. The grain offering may be fine flour with oil and frankincense or grain prepared in an oven, on a griddle or in a pan. A memorial portion is burned and the priests receive the rest. The offering is seasoned with the salt of the covenant. Leaven and honey are excluded from what is burned, and provision is made for firstfruits.~A peace offering may be a male or female from herd or flock without blemish. The worshipper lays a hand on its head, the priests dash its blood against the altar and the designated fat is burned for the Lord. The reading then turns to wrong done without intent. It gives different directions for an anointed priest, the entire congregation, a ruler and an ordinary person. Where the priest or congregation has sinned, blood is taken into the tent and applied before the veil and at the incense altar. Other cases involve the horns of the altar of burnt offering. Within these differences the same point returns: wrongdoing still requires acknowledgement and atonement even when the offender did not set out to do it.~The Lord names cases involving withheld testimony, contact with uncleanness and rash oaths. A person who becomes aware of guilt is to confess. The instructions allow a sheep or goat, two birds for a person unable to afford one, and a measured amount of flour for a person too poor for birds. The guilt offering also addresses wrong concerning holy things and wrong against neighbours, including fraud, robbery and keeping lost property while swearing falsely about it. Restitution includes the full value and an additional fifth. The wronged neighbour receives what is due before the offender brings the ram prescribed for atonement. The altar never makes the neighbour's loss disappear from the account.~The priests receive further directions for tending the fire, removing ashes and handling their portions. The altar fire is to burn continually. The passages distinguish offerings that may be eaten by the priests and their households from those whose blood is brought inside and whose flesh is burned. A thanksgiving peace offering is eaten that day, while a vow or freewill offering allows a second day. The people must refrain from consuming blood or the designated fat, and the breast and right thigh are assigned to the priests. The closing summary recalls that the Lord gave the instructions at Sinai. Ordinary people and priests alike have been told what part belongs to them and what belongs on the Lord's altar.|Approaching God involves reverence and honest acknowledgement of wrong. Where we have taken something from another person, worship gives us reason to make restitution.|What loss could you repay or what truth could you speak as part of setting a wrong right?
8:1-10:20|Fire at the Beginning of Priestly Service|Aaron and his sons are ordained, and the priesthood learns the seriousness of drawing near|The Lord tells Moses to gather the congregation at the entrance of the tent with Aaron, his sons, the garments, oil and animals required for their ordination. Moses washes Aaron and his sons. He clothes Aaron in the appointed garments, including the breastpiece, turban and gold plate. He anoints the dwelling and its furnishings and pours oil on Aaron's head. He clothes Aaron's sons, brings the bull for the sin offering and the two rams, and performs the offerings. Blood from the ram of ordination is placed on their right ears, thumbs and great toes. Moses sprinkles oil and blood on the priests and garments. They eat at the entrance and must remain there for seven days, attending to the Lord's charge.~On the eighth day Moses summons Aaron, his sons and Israel's elders. Aaron brings his sin offering and burnt offering, and the people bring theirs together with a peace offering and grain offering. Moses says the Lord's glory will appear. Aaron first offers for himself, then offers for the people. He raises his hands and blesses them. Moses and Aaron enter the tent, come out and bless the people again. The glory of the Lord appears and fire comes out from before him and consumes the offering and fat on the altar. When the congregation sees it, the people shout and fall on their faces. The ordination that occupied the previous seven days reaches its public purpose in worship the Lord receives.~Nadab and Abihu take their censers, put fire and incense in them and offer fire the Lord had not commanded. Fire comes out from before him and they die. Moses tells Aaron that the Lord will show himself holy among those who draw near and be glorified before all the people. Aaron remains silent. Relatives carry the bodies outside the camp in their garments. Aaron and his surviving sons are told to keep their appointed place and refrain from the usual outward signs of mourning while the rest of Israel laments. The contrast between the fires is plain in the sequence. The fire that consumed the offering was received with a shout. The fire that consumed the priests leaves their father without a word.~The Lord speaks directly to Aaron about abstaining from wine and strong drink when entering the tent. The priests are to distinguish holy from common and unclean from clean and teach Israel the statutes the Lord gave through Moses. Moses directs the surviving priests about their portions of the offerings. He becomes angry when he learns that the goat of the sin offering has been burned rather than eaten in the holy place. Aaron replies by pointing to what has happened that day and asking whether eating it would have pleased the Lord. Moses hears him and is satisfied. The chapter ends with careful speech between men who have had to carry out public duties on a day of grief.|People who serve before God must learn what he asks of them and teach it carefully. Aaron's reply also reminds us to hear a grieving person before judging their conduct.|In a duty entrusted to you, what would careful attention to God's instruction and another person's circumstances look like?
11:1-15:33|Cleanliness in the Camp|Israel receives practical instructions about food, bodily conditions and return to community life|The Lord tells Moses and Aaron which land animals Israel may eat. They must have a split hoof and chew the cud. The camel, hare and pig are among the exceptions named. Creatures of the waters must have fins and scales. Certain birds are forbidden, and some winged insects that leap are permitted. The law addresses touching carcasses, washing clothes and the effect on vessels, food and water. Earthen vessels may have to be broken, while a spring or cistern remains clean. Seed is treated differently depending on whether water has been put on it. These instructions reach kitchens, storage vessels and the places where people draw water. The Lord links the practice of distinguishing clean from unclean to his having brought Israel out of Egypt and to his own holiness.~After childbirth a woman observes prescribed periods of uncleanness and purification. A son is circumcised on the eighth day. When the days are complete the mother brings a lamb and a bird, or two birds if she cannot afford a lamb. The priest makes atonement and she is clean. The instructions then turn to marks on skin. The priest examines swelling, rash or bright spots, noting such things as depth, hair and whether a mark spreads. Where the condition is uncertain, he shuts the person up for a period and examines again. Some marks he declares clean. The law also distinguishes sores, burns, scalp conditions and harmless baldness, and gives tests for marks that appear in wool, linen and leather. A person pronounced unclean with the specified disease lives outside the camp and warns those who come near.~The Lord gives a route back when the condition has healed. The priest goes outside the camp and examines the person, and the first cleansing involves two birds, cedar wood, scarlet and hyssop, with one bird released. The person washes, shaves and may return to the camp, then stays outside their tent for seven days. Further washing and offerings follow. Blood and oil are applied to the right ear, thumb and great toe, with a reduced offering available for a poor person. When a mark appears in a house, its contents are removed before the priest comes. He may close the house, order affected stones taken out and have plaster scraped away. A persistent outbreak can require demolition, while a house found clean has its own cleansing rite.~The final instructions address bodily discharges in men and women, including ordinary emissions and monthly bleeding as well as continuing conditions. Contact with beds, seats and affected objects brings washing or a period of uncleanness. When specified continuing conditions cease, days are counted and birds offered. The rules distinguish conditions people may experience without moral wrongdoing from the question of fitness to approach God's dwelling. The chapter closes by saying Israel must be kept from defiling the tabernacle in their midst. The person who had to leave the camp, the family whose house was examined and the worshipper who waited through days of purification all receive concrete directions about what happens next.|God's holiness reached bodily life as well as public worship. The instructions make room for examination, waiting, affordable offerings and return after cleansing.|How could you help a person regain their place in a community after a difficult period away?
16:1-17:16|The Day the High Priest Entered the Veil|Atonement is made for the people, and blood is set apart because the life is in it|After the death of Aaron's two sons, the Lord tells Moses that Aaron may not come at any time behind the veil before the cover of the ark. The Lord appears in the cloud above it. Aaron is to come with a bull for a sin offering and a ram for a burnt offering. He bathes and puts on holy linen garments. The congregation supplies two male goats and a ram, and Aaron first presents the bull for himself and his household. He sets the two goats before the Lord at the door and casts lots, one for the Lord and one for the goat to be sent away. The high priest begins by making provision for his own household before carrying responsibility for everyone else.~Aaron kills his bull and carries burning coals and finely beaten incense within the veil. The cloud of incense covers the mercy seat. He sprinkles the bull's blood before it and then kills the goat for the people and brings its blood inside. He makes atonement for the holy place because of Israel's uncleanness and transgressions, and does the same for the tent in the middle of the camp. Nobody else is to be in the tent while he does this. He also cleanses the altar with blood. The dwelling among Israel is treated as a holy place affected by the uncleanness of the people around it. The instructions provide for cleansing the sanctuary as well as for the people whose sins have brought the need for it.~Aaron lays both hands on the head of the living goat and confesses Israel's iniquities, transgressions and sins over it. An appointed man takes it away and the goat bears the iniquities into an isolated land. Aaron leaves his linen clothes in the tent, bathes and offers the burnt offerings. The man who took the goat away and the person who burns the remains of the sin offerings outside the camp wash before returning. On the tenth day of the seventh month Israelites and resident strangers are to humble themselves and cease work. The appointed priest makes atonement for the sanctuary, priests and people each year. The day asks the whole congregation to stop ordinary work while atonement is made on its behalf.~The Lord then restricts the killing of sacrificial animals to the appointed place at the tent entrance. The people are to cease offering sacrifices to goat demons. Israelites and resident foreigners who offer a sacrifice must bring it to the Lord there. They are forbidden to eat blood because the life of the flesh is in it and God has given it on the altar to make atonement for their lives. A hunter pours out blood and covers it with dust. Anyone who eats an animal found dead or torn by beasts washes and remains unclean until evening. The reading ends where an ordinary meal can show whether a person has remembered what God said about life and blood at the sanctuary.|Atonement is God's provision for people who need cleansing. It gives no one grounds to minimise sin, including the people who lead worship.|What do you need to confess plainly before God without treating another person's wrongdoing as an excuse for your own?
18:1-20:27|Holiness at Home, in the Field and at the Gate|God calls Israel to a distinct life in intimate relationships, work, judgment and worship|The Lord tells Israel to order life by his statutes instead of following Egypt, from which they came, or Canaan, where they are going. He sets boundaries for sexual relations within families and names other prohibited acts, including adultery, relations with an animal and prohibited same-sex intercourse. He forbids giving children to Molech. The nations' conduct has defiled the land and it will expel its inhabitants, and Israel receives the same warning if it follows their ways. The Lord speaks to people about what happens in households, where conduct can be hidden from neighbours. He treats those choices as matters that shape the future of the land they are about to occupy.~The whole congregation is called to holiness because the Lord is holy. Reverence for parents and observance of Sabbaths appear beside a prohibition on idols. Peace offerings have an appointed time in which they may be eaten. Harvesters must leave the field's edges and fallen grapes for poor people and resident foreigners. The law forbids theft, lies, false oaths, withholding wages overnight, cursing the deaf and putting obstacles in the way of the blind. Judgments must be fair to both poor and powerful. Israel is forbidden to slander, endanger a neighbour's life, harbour hatred, take vengeance or bear grudges. The Lord commands love for the neighbour as for oneself. He also calls for honest correction, so that tolerating harm under the appearance of peace does not become its own fault.~Further instructions distinguish kinds of livestock, seed and cloth, and address sexual wrongdoing involving a slave woman, with an offering for the man's guilt. Fruit trees are left ungathered for three years and their fourth year's fruit is holy before the people eat in the fifth. Practices involving blood, divination, ritual cutting and prostitution are forbidden. Israel is to honour the sanctuary, keep Sabbath, refuse mediums, rise before the elderly and show reverence to God. The stranger is to be treated as native-born and loved as oneself because Israel lived as strangers in Egypt. Honest weights and measures are required. The memory of rescue from Egypt reaches the place where grain is weighed and where a stranger looks for fair treatment.~The Lord gives judgments for offering a child to Molech, turning to mediums, cursing parents and violating the boundaries already stated for marriage and family. The community must not close its eyes to a person who gives a child to Molech. The reading repeats the warning that the land can expel its inhabitants. Israel is to distinguish clean and unclean creatures and remember that the Lord has separated this people to belong to him. The final prohibition concerns mediums and spiritists. The cluster of chapters returns repeatedly to the Lord's name. The command to be holy governs what people worship, whose wages they pay, how they treat a stranger and what they do when they hold power over someone else.|God sets his people apart in their ordinary relationships and dealings. Love for the neighbour is visible in fair wages, honest measures, truthful correction and care for people easily disregarded.|Which instruction about wages, speech, judgment or hospitality shows you something to put right this week?
21:1-24:23|Holy Time, Holy Service and Equal Judgment|The priests learn their duties, Israel receives its calendar, and a case in the camp tests public justice|The Lord gives directions for the priests, including restrictions concerning mourning, marriage and contact with the dead. A priest ordinarily may attend to certain close relatives, while the high priest has stricter duties because of the anointing upon him. A descendant of Aaron with certain bodily conditions may eat the holy portions but may not approach the veil or altar to offer them. Priests who become unclean must wait and wash as instructed before eating holy food. Rules distinguish people attached to a priest's household from outside visitors and specify restitution for accidental consumption. Israelite and resident foreign worshippers are to bring acceptable animals without blemish. The Lord sets conditions about the age of an animal, slaughtering mother and young on the same day, and eating a thanksgiving offering. He reminds Israel who brought them out of Egypt.~The appointed times begin with Sabbath and continue with Passover and Unleavened Bread. When Israel enters the land, the first sheaf of harvest is brought before the Lord before the people eat from the new crop. They count seven complete weeks and bring an offering of new grain on the fiftieth day, together with sacrifices and a holy assembly. The instruction to leave field edges and gleanings for the poor and stranger stands in the same chapter. The seventh month opens with a day marked by trumpet blasts and rest. The Day of Atonement calls for self-humbling and cessation of work. The Feast of Booths follows. The people rejoice and live in booths to remember that the Lord made Israel dwell in booths when he brought them out of Egypt.~The law returns to the tent. Pure beaten olive oil is supplied so Aaron can tend the lamps before the Lord continually, from evening to morning. Twelve cakes are placed on the pure table in two rows, with frankincense, and replaced each Sabbath. Aaron and his sons eat them in a holy place. Israel's yearly gatherings, the weekly setting out of bread and the daily tending of light all give regular times for duties the people can remember and carry out. The record then moves from those duties to a dispute in the camp, where the Lord's name is misused and a case is brought before Moses.~The son of an Israelite woman and an Egyptian man fights with an Israelite. He blasphemes the name and curses, and witnesses bring him to Moses. He is held until the Lord's decision is declared. The Lord tells Moses that those who heard must lay hands on his head and the congregation must stone him outside the camp. The ruling says the same law applies to the resident foreigner and the native. It also states judgments concerning killing a person, killing an animal and inflicting injury. Israel carries out the sentence. The passage ends by applying public law to an actual dispute after a series of instructions about keeping worship, time and service holy.|God orders public worship and public justice. His people are to remember the poor at harvest and apply the same standard of judgment to the resident foreigner and the native-born.|Where would a consistent standard of fairness change the way you treat someone outside your own group?
25:1-27:34|The Land Belongs to the Lord|Sabbath years, Jubilee, covenant warnings and vows teach Israel to hold its possessions under God|The Lord tells Israel to let the land rest every seventh year. They sow and prune for six years, but what grows of itself in the seventh is available for households, servants, strangers and animals. After seven cycles of seven years, the trumpet sounds on the Day of Atonement and liberty is proclaimed in the fiftieth year. People return to family and property. The price of land takes account of harvest years remaining until Jubilee. The land belongs to the Lord, and Israel lives with him as strangers and tenants. He promises provision around the fallow year and requires a right of redemption. A relative may redeem land a poor person sold, or the seller may buy it back if able. Walled city houses and the property of Levites have distinct provisions.~If a neighbour becomes poor, Israel is to support that person without taking interest or profit on necessities. Poor Israelites who sell their labour are to serve as hired workers until Jubilee, for they are the Lord's servants brought from Egypt. The law permits acquiring slaves from surrounding peoples and distinguishes their status from that of Israelites. An Israelite sold to a prosperous resident foreigner may be redeemed by relatives or by personal means, according to years remaining. The law requires that such a person be treated as a hired worker and released at Jubilee if not redeemed earlier. The text gives protections in some cases while retaining distinctions that the reader must see plainly in the law itself.~The Lord promises rain, harvest, security and peace if the people walk in his statutes. They will have food to spare and the Lord will dwell among them. If they reject his commands, he warns of disease, failed crops, enemies and escalating calamities, including siege, desolation and scattering. The land will enjoy the Sabbaths they withheld while it lies empty. Those who survive will be fearful in the lands of their enemies. Yet if they confess their iniquity and humble their uncircumcised hearts, the Lord will remember his covenant with Jacob, Isaac and Abraham. He will remember the land and will not utterly reject or destroy his people in exile. The warnings make disobedience grave and give the scattered a reason to confess.~The final chapter assigns valuations for persons dedicated by vow, with provision for those too poor to pay the stated amount. It distinguishes clean sacrificial animals, unclean animals, houses and fields. In several cases redemption adds a fifth, and the valuation of fields accounts for Jubilee. Firstborn animals already belong to the Lord, and things devoted irrevocably are treated differently from ordinary vows. The tithe of produce belongs to the Lord and may be redeemed with an added fifth. Every tenth animal passing under the rod is holy and cannot be exchanged. Leviticus ends with these commands at Sinai. After laws about land and crop cycles, the final concern is what an individual can rightly promise and what was already God's before any promise was made.|The Lord's ownership puts limits on possession and profit. His people are responsible for their vows and for how they deal with neighbours who have fallen into hardship.|What possession, agreement or financial choice would you handle differently if you remembered that you hold it under God's authority?
