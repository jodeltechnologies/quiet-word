1:1-15|Peace on the Mountains|The Lord confronts Nineveh and promises to break Judah's yoke|Nahum's vision opens with the name of Nineveh, but before he tells what will happen to the city, he tells who is coming against it. The Lord is jealous and avenges wrong. He is slow to anger and great in power, and he will hold the guilty to account. In the prophet's picture the whirlwind is his way and the clouds are the dust of his feet. He rebukes the sea and it dries up. Rivers fail, and places known for their growth, Bashan, Carmel and Lebanon, wither before him. Mountains quake and hills melt. The city that weighed so heavily on Judah is placed in a world that itself trembles when the Lord draws near.~Nahum asks who can stand before such indignation. His next words would have been worth remembering for a family that lived under the threat of Assyria. The Lord is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble, and he knows those who take refuge in him. The same prophecy speaks of an overflowing flood that makes an end of the enemy and of darkness pursuing those who oppose him. The difference between the people seeking shelter and the people plotting evil is a matter of where each stands before the Lord. Those who had found no defence in their circumstances could still turn to him. Those who assumed their power made them untouchable had reason to fear.~The prophet addresses those who plot against the Lord. He says their trouble will be brought to an end and pictures their destruction as the burning of tangled thorns and dry stubble. One who counsels wickedness has come out of Nineveh. Even if the enemy is at full strength and numerous, the Lord says they will be cut down. Then the speech turns to Judah. The Lord acknowledges the affliction his people have known and says that he will break the yoke from their neck and tear away their bonds. The Assyrian's own future is also addressed. His name will have no line to carry it on, the images in his god's house will be cut off, and a grave is prepared for him.~The chapter ends by asking Judah to look up to the mountains. A messenger approaches with news of peace. Judah can keep her feasts and pay her vows again because the wicked invader will pass through her no more. The promise reaches into ordinary life. People who had lived under a yoke could gather for worship again and fulfil what they had vowed. The peace announced from the hills rests on the Lord's decision to deal with the adversary. Nahum places the messenger at the close of the chapter after the storm, the question about God's anger and the breaking of the bonds. The people hearing the news now know whose hand has made their freedom possible.|God knows those who take refuge in him and holds oppressive power accountable. His deliverance gives his people reason to return to worship with gratitude.|What would it look like to take refuge in God while a trouble you cannot control is still unresolved?
2:1-3:19|The City That Lived on Spoil|Nineveh loses the wealth and defences that once made other nations afraid|Nahum brings the siege into view. An attacker advances and Nineveh is told to guard the ramparts, watch the road, brace herself and gather all her strength. The Lord is restoring the splendour of Jacob and Israel after plunderers emptied them and ruined their branches. Now red shields and flashing chariots appear outside and chariots race through the streets like torches. Officers stumble as they rush towards the wall. The river gates open, the palace gives way, and women mourn as they are led away. The city that had been like a pool of water sees its people stream out. Calls to halt do nothing to turn them back. Silver and gold lie open to the plunderer. Hearts melt and knees give way in the city that once brought those fears to others.~Nahum asks where the lions' den has gone. The picture is of a lair filled with enough prey to feed the lioness and her cubs. The stockpile makes sense once we see the tearing and killing that filled it. The Lord of hosts declares himself against Nineveh. He will burn its chariots, cut off its prey and silence its messengers. The next oracle calls it a bloody city full of lies and robbery. The noise of whip, wheel, horse and chariot accompanies the sight of bodies in the road. The city's sorceries and seductions had ensnared nations. Now the Lord announces that her shame will be exposed before those nations, and he asks where anyone will find comforters for her.~Nineveh is asked to remember No, the city also called Thebes. Waters surrounded that city and served as a defence. Ethiopia and Egypt supplied strength, and other peoples helped her. She still went into captivity. Her little children were dashed in the streets, lots were cast for her honoured men, and her leaders were put in chains. Nineveh will know a similar helplessness. Nahum likens her strongholds to trees whose ripe figs fall straight into the eater's mouth when shaken. The city can draw water for the siege, strengthen her brickwork and prepare the kiln. Fire and sword will still find her there. Multiplying her merchants and officials will give her as much protection as a swarm of locusts that disappears when the sun warms it.~The final words reach the king of Assyria himself. His shepherds sleep, his nobles lie still and his people scatter over the mountains with nobody to gather them. His wound cannot be healed. Those who hear the news clap their hands, and the prophet ends by asking who escaped his continual cruelty. That question explains the response of the nations. The empty palace and scattered officials are the end of a rule that had caused suffering far beyond Nineveh. Nahum lets those who endured its violence have the last place in the account. Their relief also recalls the messenger of peace who appeared on Judah's mountains at the beginning of the book.|God judges the cruelty and deceit by which people build and protect their wealth. The people harmed by that power remain in his sight.|Where might the comfort or security you enjoy make it easy to overlook the way someone else has been treated?
