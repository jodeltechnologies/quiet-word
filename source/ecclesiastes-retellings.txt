1:1-18|The Preacher Watches the Returning Wind|What remains from a life of labour?|Ecclesiastes introduces the words of the Preacher, a son of David and king in Jerusalem. His opening cry calls everything vanity, using a word that evokes a breath or vapour, something fleeting and difficult to grasp. He asks what lasting gain a person obtains from all the labour done under the sun. The question is searching rather than casual. People give their strength and years to work, yet the result cannot be secured permanently within their hands. The book will follow this difficulty honestly, bringing its observations, grief and counsel towards the closing call to fear God and keep his commandments.~The Preacher looks at the familiar movement of the world. Generations come and go while the earth remains. The sun rises, sets and returns. The wind circles, and rivers continue flowing into a sea that never becomes full. These repeated movements place human restlessness beside patterns greater than any individual life. Eyes are not satisfied by seeing, and ears are not filled by hearing. An apparent newness may conceal something already known in former days. The emphasis is on recurring human experience, not a scientific claim that no invention or previously unseen event can ever occur. Novelty cannot by itself answer the question of lasting gain.~Memory also fails to provide the security people want. Earlier generations are forgotten, and future generations will face the same fading recognition. The Preacher then describes his own search through wisdom into everything done under heaven. He has given his heart to the task and recognises the burden of the inquiry. Looking across human works, he finds them fleeting and like chasing the wind. What is crooked cannot simply be straightened by wishing, and what is missing cannot be counted as though it were present. The search exposes limits that neither status nor effort can remove.~His considerable wisdom has not lifted him beyond those limits. He compares wisdom with madness and folly, yet finds that the inquiry itself brings vexation. Increased knowledge can increase sorrow because a clearer sight reveals more of what is broken. The chapter does not ask readers to despise learning. It challenges the expectation that enough information will make a human being master of life. The royal speaker has resources to investigate, but he cannot turn understanding into complete control. His question remains open at this point in the book. Readers are invited to listen through the whole argument rather than mistake an early expression of frustration for the final account of life before God.|Human effort and knowledge are real gifts, yet neither can provide complete control or lasting security. The book's questions should be heard within its whole movement towards reverence for God.|What are you asking an achievement or a new experience to give you that it cannot permanently secure?
2:1-26|Houses, Gardens and a Pleasure That Cannot Last|Receiving good work as a gift rather than a final gain|The Preacher tests pleasure as another possible answer to his question. He considers laughter, enjoyment and wine while describing wisdom as still guiding the experiment. He undertakes great projects, building houses, planting vineyards and establishing gardens, orchards and pools. He acquires livestock, silver, gold, singers and other royal delights. Servants, including people born within his household, appear within the account of his wealth and power. Their inclusion describes the ancient royal setting. It should not make ownership of people appear morally admirable. The scale of the experiment is meant to show that the speaker has had abundant opportunity to test what ordinary ambition promises.~He withholds little that his eyes desire and recognises a measure of joy within the work itself. Yet when he turns to examine the whole achievement, he finds no permanent gain that can be held beyond the passing life. Pleasure has been experienced, and projects have been completed, but the deeper problem remains. The distinction matters. He does not say that a garden can never be beautiful or that work can never provide satisfaction. He says that these goods cannot bear the weight of becoming an enduring answer to human mortality and restless desire. Possession has not supplied the permanence he sought.~Wisdom is better than folly as light is better than darkness. Nevertheless, both the wise and the foolish die and may be forgotten. The Preacher's grief becomes intense. He hates the labour whose fruits must be left to someone who did not perform it and may handle it foolishly. An inheritance can pass beyond the maker's judgement and control. Anxiety can continue into the night, depriving the worker of rest even before the work ends. These statements describe the anguish of his investigation. They are not a command to despise life, harm oneself or abandon every responsibility because its outcome cannot be guaranteed.~A different note then enters. Eating, drinking and finding enjoyment in work are recognised as gifts from the hand of God. The good is received within a limited life rather than seized as a final conquest over its limits. Wisdom, knowledge and joy depend upon God's generosity. The chapter also pictures the sinner gathering possessions that may pass to another. Accumulation remains unstable when treated as a source of mastery. The Preacher has moved through pleasure, great works, wealth, comparison with folly and the difficulty of inheritance. His first constructive counsel asks the reader to receive daily provision and worthwhile labour gratefully, acknowledging the giver instead of demanding that the gift make him independent of God.|Work and pleasure become burdens when expected to guarantee permanence. Their proper enjoyment grows through gratitude to God and an honest acceptance of human limits.|What part of your work could you receive with gratitude while releasing the demand to control its whole future?
3:1-22|A Time for Weeping and a Time for Laughter|God's work is greater than the season we can see|The Preacher sets human life within a series of changing times. Birth and death, planting and uprooting, breaking and building, weeping and laughter, mourning and dancing all have their seasons. The poem continues through gathering and scattering, embracing and refraining, seeking and losing, keeping and discarding, silence and speech, love and hate, war and peace. Its pairs encompass the varied experience of life under heaven. Naming a time to kill or hate does not turn every violent intention or bitter feeling into God's approval. The poem observes the range of events and responses that people meet, including painful realities they cannot simply schedule away.~The question of gain returns. God has given human beings work and concern, yet the whole pattern of his activity lies beyond their sight. Everything has its fitting time, while people have a sense of a larger whole that they cannot fully discover from beginning to end. This tension explains part of their restlessness. They long to understand more than the present moment allows. The Preacher commends doing good and receiving enjoyment in life, food, drink and work as God's gift. What God does endures beyond the changes that frustrate human possession, and this calls people to reverence rather than mastery.~The chapter then notices wickedness where justice should be found. The existence of seasons does not make corruption morally neutral. The Preacher affirms that God will judge both righteous and wicked, with a time for every purpose and work. The judgement that human courts fail to provide remains a matter before God. His observation also confronts human pride. Death comes to people and animals, and both return to dust. Breath and mortal fragility reveal a shared creaturely dependence. Human status cannot prevent the body from reaching the earth from which it came.~Questions about the spirit of human beings and animals expose what can be established from ordinary earthly observation. These questions should be read alongside the book's later statement that the spirit returns to God and its final affirmation of judgement. A single question here does not provide the whole biblical account of life after death. The Preacher returns to the good available within a person's present work because no human observer can guarantee seeing what follows. The chapter therefore joins changing seasons, enjoyment, injustice and mortality. Its invitation is to do good within the time given, receive God's gifts and leave the complete knowledge of his enduring work in his hands.|Changing seasons call for discernment and reverence. We can do good and receive God's gifts without claiming to understand his whole work.|What does faithfulness require in your present season, rather than in the season you wish had already arrived?
4:1-16|The Tears of the Oppressed and the Help of a Friend|Life suffers when power and labour leave people alone|The Preacher turns again to oppression and sees the tears of people who have no comforter. Power stands with their oppressors, while relief is absent. The repetition makes their loneliness difficult to overlook. He expresses the horror of such suffering through the thought that the dead, or those never born, seem better placed than people forced to witness this evil. These are words of lament within his investigation of life under the sun. They neither praise self-harm nor excuse a society that leaves the oppressed without help. The passage asks readers to take the anguish seriously enough to notice where power is being misused.~He then considers work driven by rivalry. A person labours partly because a neighbour's success arouses envy, and achievement becomes another way to chase the wind. Yet the opposite extreme, folding the hands in idleness, consumes the person's own well-being. The saying about one handful with quietness and two handfuls with exhausting striving offers a more measured course. The problem is neither every effort nor every aspiration. It is the restless demand to outdo someone else until labour loses its place within a good life. Contentment can require refusing an unnecessary gain that carries a larger cost in peace.~Another worker is completely alone, with neither child nor brother, yet continues accumulating without satisfaction. He fails to ask for whom he is working or why he denies himself enjoyment. The description exposes the loneliness hidden beneath an apparently successful routine. The next sayings answer with the value of companionship. Two have a good return for their work. One can lift the other after a fall, offer warmth and help withstand attack. A cord of three strands is difficult to break. The immediate point is the strength of shared life. The passage does not restrict this help to marriage or require identifying each strand as a particular spiritual figure.~The final scene compares a poor but wise young person with an old king who refuses further counsel. A change of position can bring the younger person from confinement to rule, and crowds may welcome the successor. Yet another generation may tire of him too. Public enthusiasm is as unstable as other forms of earthly gain. The chapter has moved from helpless victims to isolated workers and celebrated rulers, showing different ways that a person can be left without lasting support. Wisdom values faithful company, receives correction and considers whom work actually serves. These modest acts of care answer needs that neither rivalry nor popularity can satisfy.|Wisdom makes room for faithful companionship and notices people left without comfort. Work should serve life and love rather than endless rivalry or isolated accumulation.|Who needs your steady presence, and what ambition may be keeping you too occupied to offer it?
5:1-20|Few Words Before God and Rest from the Love of Money|Reverent worship and grateful provision|The Preacher warns the worshipper to approach God's house ready to listen. A sacrifice offered without considering one's evil is the offering of a fool. Words must not rush ahead of reverence, because God is in heaven and the speaker is on earth. The point is not that prayer must be emotionally distant or always extremely brief. It is that many words cannot replace a truthful, listening heart. Just as much activity can trouble sleep with dreams, a flood of speech can expose folly. Worship begins with attention to the God being addressed rather than confidence in the performance of the address.~Vows require particular care. A promise made to God should be fulfilled, and it is better to refrain from promising than to promise and refuse to pay. Calling the words a mistake after their cost becomes clear does not make them weightless. The warning concerns sincere, responsible worship, not the fulfilment of a sinful promise that should never be carried out. The chapter gathers its counsel into the fear of God. Reverence steadies speech and conduct together. Dreams, declarations and religious activity become empty when they allow a person to avoid the obedience already due.~The subject then turns to oppression within a province and layers of authority above it. Corruption and distorted justice may operate through systems rather than through one isolated offender. The observer should not be naive about them, yet no king stands independent of the field that supplies his food. Wealth is similarly exposed as dependent and limited. Someone who loves silver does not become satisfied merely by gaining more silver. Increasing possessions also increases claims upon them. The labourer's sleep can be sweet, while an abundance that seems to promise security may keep its owner awake with anxiety.~The Preacher has seen riches kept to the owner's hurt and fortunes lost so that a son receives nothing. A person leaves life as empty-handed as he entered it, unable to carry his labour away. Darkness, resentment, sickness and grief can accompany a life devoted to grasping. Against this sorrow stands the good of receiving food, drink and enjoyment within the days God gives. Both provision and the capacity to enjoy it are gifts. The chapter brings worship and money under the same correction. Neither abundant speech nor abundant possessions can make a person master before God. A listening heart, faithful promises and gratitude for present provision offer a wiser course than religious display or accumulation without rest.|Reverence makes worship truthful and possessions less controlling. Both the promise we make and the provision we receive belong before God.|Where could fewer claims, a kept commitment or simpler gratitude make your response to God more honest?
6:1-12|Having Much and Enjoying Little|The wandering desire and the shadow of a brief life|The Preacher describes another painful situation. Someone receives wealth, possessions and honour, apparently lacking nothing he desires, yet cannot enjoy them. A stranger receives the benefit instead. The account names this a serious sorrow. It exposes the difference between possessing something and having the opportunity or capacity to enjoy it. The book has already called enjoyment a gift from God, and the contrast now makes that dependence clear. An impressive inventory cannot by itself supply health, peace, a secure future or a satisfied heart. The person looking in from outside may therefore misunderstand the actual condition of a life that seems enviable.~The thought is extended through deliberately large numbers. A man might have a hundred children and live many years, yet never experience the good he sought or even receive an honourable burial. The Preacher compares this miserable course with a child who dies before seeing life. His severe comparison expresses grief at a life emptied of enjoyment. It should not be read as dismissing the sorrow of stillbirth, denying the child's worth or recommending death as a solution to suffering. Even two thousand years would fail to answer the problem if the person could never receive good. Length alone cannot establish the quality or meaning of the life lived.~Human labour feeds the mouth, yet appetite can remain unfilled. The Preacher questions what wisdom, social skill or poverty can finally change about that restless condition. He commends the sight of what is present over the wandering of desire. This is an invitation to recognise a good actually given rather than endlessly pursuing what is absent. It does not require accepting injustice without seeking help or refusing a necessary improvement. It challenges a heart that continually moves its imagined point of satisfaction further away. The desired possession becomes ordinary as soon as it is gained, and another object takes its place.~The closing questions return to human limits. People cannot contend successfully with the One mightier than themselves, and multiplying words does not necessarily improve their condition. Who can fully know what is good throughout a brief life passing like a shadow, or tell someone with certainty what will happen after him under the sun? The questions prepare the learner for humility. They leave room for responsible plans and moral knowledge while refusing a claim to complete foresight. A grateful person can receive what is good today and seek wisdom for tomorrow without pretending to control the whole. The chapter's sorrow calls attention to the heart's capacity for endless desire and the mercy of learning to receive enough.|Possessions and long life cannot guarantee a satisfied heart. Gratitude notices the good already given while humility acknowledges what remains beyond our control.|What present good are you overlooking because your desire keeps moving towards what you do not yet have?
7:1-29|The House of Mourning and the Limits of Wisdom|Learning from sorrow without pretending to know everything|A good name is valued above costly perfume, and the day of death is set beside the day of birth. The comparison leads to a house of mourning, where the living must consider the end common to everyone. The Preacher values this seriousness above a feast that keeps mortality out of sight. His words do not forbid joy or declare that grief is always morally superior. They expose the learning that may be avoided when amusement fills every silence. The rebuke of a wise person can do more good than the noisy song of fools, whose laughter is compared to the quick crackling of thorns beneath a pot.~Oppression and bribery can damage judgement, while patience is better than pride. The learner must not welcome sudden anger or assume that former days were necessarily better. Wisdom and money can both provide some protection, but wisdom has a life-preserving value that possessions alone cannot give. Prosperity may be enjoyed, and adversity requires consideration. God's work cannot be forced into a simple pattern under human command. This becomes especially clear when a righteous person dies while a wicked person continues living. The observation resists the claim that every immediate outcome is a transparent report on someone's moral condition.~The difficult warning against being excessively righteous or wise should be heard within this exposure of human pretension. It is not permission to reserve a comfortable share of wickedness. The passage also warns against evil and folly and directs the hearer towards the fear of God. Self-righteous overconfidence cannot make life controllable, while wrongdoing brings its own danger. No person on earth does good without sinning. The listener should also avoid taking every spoken slight to heart, remembering his own history of speaking wrongly about others. Humility receives correction and recognises personal fault rather than demanding an innocence it cannot honestly claim.~The Preacher continues searching for wisdom and acknowledges that its depths remain beyond him. He describes the danger of a seductive woman whose heart is a trap, then gives a troubling account of his unsuccessful search among men and women. This limited search must not become a claim that women have less human worth or are incapable of wisdom. Proverbs has already honoured women's teaching and character. The chapter's final statement concerns humanity together. God made people upright, but they have pursued many schemes. The reader should carry that shared responsibility into the passage's difficult observations, receiving its call to humility without turning another person's sex into an excuse for contempt.|Sorrow can teach us to live more honestly, while the fear of God corrects self-righteous certainty. Human limitations should deepen humility towards others.|What truth about your limits or your own failures would help you receive correction more patiently?
8:1-17|Power, Delayed Justice and the Work We Cannot Trace|Reverence persists when outcomes remain difficult to explain|Wisdom gives a person a changed bearing and helps him consider the meaning of a situation. The Preacher applies it to conduct before a king, where an oath and the ruler's real power require seriousness. Hasty departure or persistence in an evil cause can bring danger. A wise heart considers the proper time and judgement. These observations belong to life under royal authority. They do not make rulers infallible or grant them the right to command disobedience to God. The same chapter will recognise the harm caused when one person exercises power over another. Wisdom must therefore be read with moral discernment, not reduced to unquestioning admiration of force.~The uncertainty of the future limits both subject and ruler. No person controls the breath at death or can arrange release from that final conflict by status alone. Wickedness cannot provide escape. The Preacher has observed people ruling others to their hurt. He has also seen the unsettling relationship between public respectability, religious places and the memory of people who acted wickedly. Appearances and reputation may fail to give a truthful account. The observer cannot assume that honour shown in a city corresponds neatly to God's judgement of the person's life.~A delayed sentence encourages some people to become more determined in evil. The absence of an immediate penalty can be mistaken for permission. The Preacher insists that it will be well with those who fear God and that wickedness lacks an enduring security before him, even when a sinner appears to enjoy many days. Yet he immediately acknowledges another difficulty. Righteous people sometimes receive what seems suited to the wicked, while wicked people receive what seems suited to the righteous. The tension is left visible. Trust in God's moral judgement does not require pretending that present circumstances consistently display it in a way everyone can read.~Enjoying food, drink and the good available within one's labour is again commended as a gift for the days God gives. The counsel stands within unresolved questions rather than after their complete solution. The Preacher searches deeply, watching work and thought that continue day and night, yet admits that the whole work of God cannot be discovered through human effort. Even a wise claim to know must meet this limit. The chapter calls the reader to reverence, responsible conduct and honest enjoyment without the pretence of a full explanation. God remains worthy of fear when injustice is delayed, power is misused and the present pattern cannot be traced to its end.|Delayed justice is no permission for evil, and confusing circumstances do not erase accountability to God. Wisdom trusts him without pretending to explain all his work.|How can you pursue what is right while leaving an unresolved outcome in God's hands?
9:1-18|The Poor Wise Man Whom Nobody Remembered|Life is limited, so present faithfulness matters|The Preacher reflects that righteous and wise people, together with their work, are in God's hand. Their present circumstances do not provide a simple way to read divine love or hatred. Death comes to people across moral and religious distinctions, and this common end troubles his observation of life under the sun. Human hearts contain evil and folly, and no earthly standing can remove mortality. Yet the living still have opportunity. A living dog is said to be better than a dead lion because life, however unimpressive it may seem, leaves room for hope and action that an earthly reputation alone cannot supply.~The statements about the dead focus on their loss of participation in what is done under the sun. Their former loves, hates, rivalries and opportunities no longer shape daily earthly activity as before. These verses should not be isolated as a complete account of the soul or allowed to cancel the book's later words about the spirit returning to God and final judgement. Their immediate force is practical. The time in which a person can do the present work is limited. Acknowledging that limit should awaken attention rather than provide a reason to abandon the responsibilities and relationships still within reach.~The Preacher therefore commends receiving ordinary goods with joy, food, suitable clothing, care of the body and life with the wife one loves. Work should be done with real effort while the opportunity remains. This is grateful participation in a passing life, not an invitation to intoxication or careless self-indulgence. The next observation also restrains pride. Races do not always go to the swift, battles to the strong, wealth to the discerning or favour to the skilled. Unexpected events interrupt the neat connection people imagine between ability and outcome. Sudden trouble may catch a person like a fish in a net.~A short account gives wisdom a striking form. A little city with few inhabitants is besieged by a powerful king. A poor wise man delivers it by his wisdom, but afterwards no one remembers him. The passage supplies no name or detailed military strategy, so these should not be invented. Its point is that wisdom can accomplish what force cannot, while poverty causes the wise speaker to be disregarded. Quiet wise words are better than the shouting of a ruler among fools, and wisdom exceeds weapons of war. Yet one sinner can undo much good. The reader is asked to value faithful understanding even when public recognition fails to follow it.|Present life offers opportunities for joy, work and service that should be used gratefully. Wisdom remains valuable when its bearer receives little recognition.|Whose quiet wisdom might you be overlooking because that person lacks money, position or public honour?
10:1-20|The Blunt Axe and the Leaking Roof|Small folly can damage a life of useful work|Dead flies spoil a perfumer's ointment, and a little folly can damage a reputation for wisdom and honour. The opening comparison warns that a small wrong is not harmless merely because it is small. A person's settled judgement shows in the direction of the heart and eventually becomes visible in conduct. Before an angry ruler, calmness can prevent a hasty response from making matters worse. The Preacher also observes a public disorder in which folly receives high position while people who appear fit for honour are lowered. The scene criticises poor judgement in the distribution of responsibility. It does not make inherited social rank a sufficient measure of wisdom.~The next sayings turn to ordinary hazards. Digging a pit, breaking through a wall, quarrying stone and splitting wood can all bring injury. The worker's activity requires knowledge and care, not merely energy. A blunt axe demands greater force unless its edge is sharpened. Wisdom is useful because it helps direct labour effectively. This image provides a practical correction to needless exhaustion. More effort is not always the answer when a tool, method or decision requires attention. The reference to a serpent biting before it is charmed likewise highlights the failure of useful skill when it is applied too late.~Speech reveals a similar difference. A wise person's words are gracious, while a fool's lips bring trouble upon himself. His talk may begin in foolishness and develop into harmful madness. He multiplies confident claims about a future no human being can fully announce. The weary labour of fools is pictured through someone unable even to find the way to the city. The comparison exposes the mismatch between pretension and practical understanding. Wisdom must remain useful in the task before a person rather than becoming a long account of matters he claims to control while ordinary responsibilities are neglected.~Leadership again affects a whole country. Rulers devoted to untimely feasting and drunkenness bring woe, while disciplined leaders eat for strength at a fitting time. A neglected building decays, and idle hands allow a roof to leak. A saying about money's usefulness recognises its broad practical role in providing for ordinary needs. It must be read alongside the book's repeated exposure of wealth's limits, not as a claim that money answers every spiritual or moral question. The closing warning about private curses reaching powerful ears calls for sober restraint in speech. Across the chapter, wisdom shows through proportion, timing, skilled work and self-control. Neglect and boastful folly can damage goods that took much longer to establish.|Wisdom directs effort through skill, timing and disciplined attention. Small neglected duties and careless words can create damage larger than they first appear.|What tool, method or neglected responsibility needs attention before you simply add more effort?
11:1-10|Sowing While the Wind Still Blows|Generosity and work within an uncertain future|The Preacher opens with the image of casting bread upon the waters and finding it after many days. The picture has been understood in relation to generosity and to ventures whose return is delayed. The following counsel encourages sharing portions broadly because a person cannot know what trouble may come upon the earth. The common direction is to act with open-handed wisdom rather than cling to a single imagined guarantee. This is no scheme by which an offering compels God to deliver a larger financial return. The chapter recognises uncertainty and asks how a person can live usefully within it.~Clouds filled with rain will empty, and a fallen tree lies where it falls. Some events arrive beyond the observer's control and cannot simply be reversed. Yet waiting for perfect weather prevents sowing and reaping. A person who studies the wind indefinitely may never put seed into the ground. Prudence considers conditions, but fear can disguise inaction as endless preparation. The learner is being invited to accept enough uncertainty to do the work that can be done. Complete foresight is unavailable, so making it a condition of obedience or useful labour means that the labour will always remain postponed.~The mystery of wind or spirit and the growth of a child within the womb remind the observer of knowledge he does not possess. God's whole work is beyond his discovery. Therefore he should sow in the morning and continue working in the evening, because he cannot know which effort will prosper or whether both will do well. The counsel commends steady, varied diligence rather than frantic activity without rest. It encourages a willingness to attempt worthwhile work without insisting that the result first become certain. The hiddenness of outcomes is a reason to remain humble and active, not a reason to become paralysed.~Light is sweet, and seeing the sun is a good to be enjoyed. Even a long life should be received with joy while remembering the days of darkness and the passing nature of what lies ahead. Young people are especially encouraged to rejoice in their youth, with the clear reminder that God will bring their choices into judgement. Enjoyment and accountability stand together. The heart's delight cannot become a warrant for evil, and future seriousness should not erase gratitude for present life. The chapter moves from bread and seed to the energy of youth, asking the learner to receive opportunities while they remain. Faithfulness involves generous action, responsible work and joy governed by remembrance of the Creator.|Uncertainty does not remove the call to work, share and rejoice responsibly. God's judgement gives present opportunities moral weight.|What worthwhile act have you postponed because you are waiting for conditions to become completely certain?
12:1-14|Remember Your Creator Before the Silver Cord Breaks|The whole inquiry ends before God's judgement|The Preacher calls the young person to remember the Creator before difficult days arrive and the years lose the ease and pleasure once taken for granted. The invitation concerns present loyalty, not a passing thought about God reserved for an older age. A poem then gathers images of failing light, returning clouds and a household whose strength diminishes. Keepers tremble, strong men bend, grinders become few and those looking through windows grow dim. Doors close, the sound of grinding lowers, sleep becomes light and music fades. The imagery evokes the frailty of age through a house and its occupants rather than offering a precise medical description for every line.~Fear attends the way, desire weakens and the mourner's procession approaches the person's long home. The images of a loosened silver cord, a broken golden bowl, a shattered pitcher and a broken wheel show life reaching a point beyond repair by ordinary human effort. Dust returns to the earth, and the spirit returns to God who gave it. The poem treats the body and its decline with solemn attention. It is no reason to despise older people or assume that their lives have no value. Its force is directed towards the living listener who might keep postponing reverence as though strength and opportunity were guaranteed indefinitely.~The refrain about vanity returns, and the book looks back upon the Preacher's work. He taught knowledge, considered and arranged proverbs, and sought fitting words that were truthful and upright. Wise words are compared to goads that direct and nails that hold firmly, given from one shepherd. The comparison recognises that genuine instruction can both disturb and steady a person. A further warning names the endless making of books and the weariness of much study. This does not dismiss education. It cautions against a pursuit of words so endless that the learner never receives the direction they are meant to provide.~The conclusion brings the whole inquiry before God. Fear him and keep his commandments, because this is the duty of humanity. Every work, including what remains secret, will come into judgement, whether good or evil. The ending must shape the reading of the frustrations and questions that preceded it. The book has not established that nothing matters. It has exposed the limits of pleasure, wealth, knowledge, reputation and control so that life can be received truthfully before its giver. The call is to remember the Creator while one can act, enjoy his gifts with gratitude, care about injustice and live with the seriousness of an account that cannot finally be hidden from him.|Remembering the Creator belongs to the present, while strength and opportunity remain. God's judgement gives meaning and accountability to even the hidden parts of life.|What act of obedience should you stop postponing as though there will always be more time?
