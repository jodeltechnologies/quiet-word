1:1-2:10|The Prophet Who Went Down to the Sea|Flight, rescue and a prayer from the depths|The Lord sent Jonah son of Amittai to Nineveh because the wickedness of the city had come before him. Jonah went down to Joppa and paid for passage on a ship bound for Tarshish. His choice gave his refusal a direction, a price and a place among people who knew nothing about the commission he had received. When the Lord sent a great wind, the ship was in danger of breaking apart. The sailors cried to their gods and threw cargo overboard. Jonah lay asleep below deck until the captain came down and urged him to call on his God, in case God might take notice and spare them.~The sailors cast lots and the lot fell on Jonah. Their questions brought out what his departure had concealed. He was a Hebrew who feared the Lord, the God of heaven, who made the sea and the dry land. The men were terrified when they heard that he was fleeing from the Lord. As the sea grew rougher, Jonah told them to throw him overboard. They rowed hard to reach land first. When their efforts failed, they asked the Lord to spare them and to hold them innocent of his blood. The sea grew calm after they lifted Jonah into it, and the sailors feared the Lord, offered a sacrifice and made vows.~The Lord appointed a great fish to swallow Jonah, and Jonah was inside it for three days and three nights. His prayer recalled a descent through water, with waves passing above him and weeds wrapping around his head. He described sinking to the roots of the mountains and feeling shut away as if the earth had barred its gates behind him. Yet in that place he remembered the Lord. His prayer reached God's holy temple. The man who had been trying to get away from the presence of the Lord now spoke of looking again towards the place associated with that presence. He credited God with bringing his life up from the pit.~Jonah contrasted clinging to worthless idols with giving thanks to the Lord and keeping his vows. He confessed that salvation belongs to the Lord. At God's command the fish released Jonah onto dry land, the very ground that Jonah had confessed God made. The rescue gives him another opportunity to hear the command he left behind at the beginning of the book. It also prepares for the harder question that follows. Jonah is deeply conscious of his need for mercy when he is sinking. How he responds when the same God shows mercy to the city to which he was sent will expose what he still has to learn.|God can reach us in the consequences of our flight and give us another opportunity to obey him.|What responsibility have you tried to travel away from, and what would it mean to return to it?
3:1-4:11|The City Jonah Wanted God to Destroy|Repentance in Nineveh and a lesson outside its walls|The word of the Lord came to Jonah a second time, sending him to Nineveh with the message God would give him. Jonah went. The book describes Nineveh as a great city, a journey of three days. Jonah began to go into it and announced that in forty days it would be overthrown. The people believed God and proclaimed a fast. Sackcloth spread among them from the greatest to the least. When the news reached the king, he stepped down from his throne, removed his robe and sat in ashes. His decree extended the fast to the animals and called on the people to turn from their evil ways and the violence in their hands.~The decree expressed hope that God might turn from his fierce anger and spare them. God saw their deeds, especially their turning from their evil way, and withheld the disaster. Jonah was furious. He prayed that this was the very outcome he had feared at home, the reason he had fled towards Tarshish. He knew the Lord was gracious and compassionate, slow to anger and abundant in steadfast love. Knowledge that could have made him grateful instead became the grounds of his complaint. He asked to die, and the Lord asked whether he was right to be angry. Jonah left the city and sat under a shelter east of it to see what would happen.~God appointed a plant that grew above Jonah and shaded his head. Jonah was exceedingly glad about it. At dawn the next day God appointed a worm that attacked the plant, and it withered. Then came a scorching east wind and a sun that beat down on Jonah until he grew faint. Again he wished to die. God asked whether he was right to be angry about the plant, and Jonah defended his anger. The sequence brought his feelings into the open with unusual precision. He had been angry at the preservation of a city and was now angry at the loss of a plant. He felt the loss immediately because the shade had been his.~The Lord pointed out that Jonah had neither laboured for the plant nor caused it to grow. It had appeared in a night and perished in a night, yet Jonah cared about it. The Lord then asked whether he should pity Nineveh, with more than one hundred and twenty thousand people who could not distinguish their right hand from their left, and also many animals. The book closes with that question. The reader has seen sailors praying for a life, a prophet rescued from drowning and a city turn from violence. Now the reader must reckon with the breadth of the compassion that made all three scenes possible.|The mercy we welcome for ourselves should teach us to desire repentance and restoration for others.|Whose repentance would you find difficult to welcome, and what does Jonah's final conversation reveal about that response?
