1:1-31|Hands Lifted in Prayer and Stained with Blood|The Holy One calls his people to return|Isaiah's vision concerns Judah and Jerusalem during the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah. The opening summons calls heaven and earth to hear the Lord's complaint. He has brought up children who have rebelled against him. Even farm animals recognise where their food comes from, while his people fail to consider their relationship with their God. Their condition is pictured as a body covered with untreated wounds and a country damaged by invasion. Zion survives like a fragile shelter in a field. Its remaining life depends upon the Lord's preservation of a remnant, not upon a national innocence it can confidently claim.~The charge becomes especially searching when it reaches worship. Sacrifices, incense, festivals and repeated prayers have continued, but violence has continued with them. Hands lifted before God are stained with blood. The problem is not that the Lord has forgotten the worship he commanded. His people have tried to place religious activity beside unrepented evil as though the two could coexist without contradiction. He commands them to wash, cease wrongdoing and learn to do good. Seeking justice means relieving the oppressed and attending to the cases of the fatherless and widow, whose needs powerful people have been able to ignore.~An invitation follows the accusation. Though their sins are scarlet, cleansing is offered with the whiteness of snow and wool. The passage does not minimise the wrong in order to make forgiveness easier. It names the blood and then offers a real return. Willing obedience and continued rebellion lead in different directions. Jerusalem, once associated with justice, has become a place of murder and corruption. Its rulers love bribes and companionship with thieves, while the vulnerable cannot get their cases heard. The deterioration is compared to silver mixed with waste and wine weakened with water, an outward name surviving after its substance has been corrupted.~The Lord announces judgement that will also refine. He will remove the dross, restore just judges and renew the city's identity as faithful. Zion's redemption is joined to righteousness, while those persisting in rebellion face destruction. The final images of withered trees, dry gardens and easily kindled material expose the weakness of false worship and proud strength. The whole opening chapter establishes themes that will recur throughout Isaiah. God's holiness confronts public injustice, religious privilege cannot shelter wrongdoing, and mercy calls for an actual turning of life. The first response is therefore more than another meeting or longer prayer. It is a return whose truth becomes visible in the treatment of a neighbour.|God receives no religious substitute for justice and repentance. His offer of cleansing calls for an obedient response that protects those easily ignored.|Whose need for justice or practical care should become part of your response to God today?
2:1-4:6|The Mountain of Peace and the Humbled City|The Lord alone will be exalted|Isaiah looks towards a day when the mountain of the Lord's house is established and nations stream towards it. People come to learn God's ways and walk in his paths. His judgement settles disputes, and weapons are remade into tools for cultivating the earth. The vision's peace grows from instruction and justice, not merely the absence of open fighting. The house of Jacob is therefore called to walk in the Lord's light now. This invitation stands beside a very different present, a land filled with wealth, military resources, foreign divination and idols fashioned by human hands. Possessions have multiplied while trust has become misplaced.~The day of the Lord brings proud height low. Trees, mountains, towers, fortified walls and impressive ships furnish the pictures of what human confidence regards as unassailable. People hide in rocky places and discard the idols that cannot protect them. Human breath itself is fragile, so a mortal person cannot carry the final weight of trust. Jerusalem's social order is then pictured collapsing as food, skilled leadership, judgement and counsel are removed. People grasp for anyone who seems able to govern, but the ruin is too great for appearances to heal. Open wrongdoing has undermined the relationships on which public life depends.~The Lord brings a case against leaders who have consumed the vineyard and stored the possessions of the poor in their houses. Grinding vulnerable people down is an offence before him. Proud display among Zion's women is also confronted, with luxurious ornaments and carefully maintained appearances set beside the humiliation of war and bereavement. The point is the judgement of arrogance within a corrupt society, not a licence to insult women or assume their appearance explains every national disaster. The loss of men in war leaves a desperate imbalance, described through women seeking the security of one man's name. This is a picture of distress, not a command establishing a marital ideal.~The vision does not end with desolation. The Lord's branch and the land's fruit appear glorious for the survivors. Those remaining in Zion are called holy after cleansing from bloodshed and impurity. Cloud by day and fire by night recall the protective presence that guided Israel in the wilderness. A shelter provides shade from heat and refuge from storm. Judgement removes false confidence, while restored life depends upon God's cleansing and care. The passage joins the wide promise of nations learning peace with the searching work required within Jerusalem itself. A city cannot welcome the Lord's peace while defending the pride and injustice his light exposes.|Lasting peace belongs with God's instruction, justice and cleansing. Pride in wealth, power or appearance cannot provide the refuge his presence gives.|What form of outward confidence needs to yield to a more humble walk in God's light?

Images of consuming fire, withered roots and dust replace the hoped-for fruit. An army is summoned from far away, its disciplined speed and readiness contrasting with the people's careless confidence. Isaiah supplies a warning rather than an invitation for readers to celebrate war. The vineyard song asks what God's care has actually produced in those who received it. The scene joins majesty, confession, cleansing, willingness and costly obedience. The prophet does not control the result, but he has encountered the Lord whose holiness exposes sin and whose mercy equips a messenger to serve.
