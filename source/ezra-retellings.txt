1:1-11|Cyrus Opens the Way Back to Jerusalem|God's word outlasts the years of exile|Ezra begins where Chronicles ended. In Cyrus's first year as king of Persia, the Lord stirred him to issue a proclamation, fulfilling the word spoken through Jeremiah. The message was announced throughout the kingdom and recorded in writing. Cyrus acknowledged the God of heaven as the source of his dominion and declared that he had been charged to build God's house in Jerusalem. Those belonging to God's people were invited to return and undertake the work. The ruined city was still a real place requiring labour, yet its future was no longer described only through the defeat that had emptied it.~The proclamation also addressed those who would remain where they lived. They were to assist returning neighbours with silver, gold, goods and animals, in addition to voluntary offerings for God's house. Restoration would involve both travelling and supplying. Family heads from Judah and Benjamin, priests, Levites and others whose spirits God stirred prepared to go. The same chapter that describes God moving a king also describes Him moving His people. Royal permission opened an opportunity, but willing households had to act upon it. Their neighbours strengthened their hands with valuable objects, supplies and animals. The return became a shared undertaking carried through practical gifts as well as a public decree.~Cyrus brought out the temple vessels that Nebuchadnezzar had removed from Jerusalem and placed in the house of his gods. Mithredath the treasurer counted them out to Sheshbazzar, the prince of Judah. The inventory names gold and silver dishes, bowls and other utensils. Objects associated with conquest were now entrusted to a journey of restoration. Their value made careful accounting necessary, but their significance went beyond the precious metal. They belonged to the worship that had been violently interrupted. Returning them testified that Babylon's seizure was not the final event in their history or in the history of the people who would use them.~The chapter gives a total of five thousand four hundred gold and silver vessels brought by Sheshbazzar with the returning exiles. It ends with movement towards Jerusalem rather than a finished temple. The people had received permission, resources and entrusted property, but the work still lay ahead. God's faithfulness did not remove the need to leave familiar surroundings, organise a journey and begin building among ruins. The opening therefore joins promise to response. A foreign king's decree, a family's willingness and a neighbour's gift all belong within the Lord's fulfilment of His word. The reader is prepared to follow that fulfilment through delay and difficulty, without mistaking those later troubles for proof that the beginning was empty.|God's faithfulness can open a new beginning after long loss. 2:1-70|The Returning Families Are Counted by Name|Restoration belongs to a whole community|The return from Babylon is recorded through families, towns and responsibilities. People carried away under Nebuchadnezzar now came again to Jerusalem and Judah, each to their own town. Zerubbabel and Jeshua appear among the leaders, followed by the names and numbers of households. Some groups number in thousands and others only dozens. The long list gives the return a human shape. It was not simply a change in imperial policy or the movement of two famous leaders. Descendants of particular families made the journey, bringing their remembered places and relationships into a land where a new life would have to be established.~The record includes people associated with Bethlehem, Anathoth, Bethel, Ai, Jericho and other settlements. Priestly families follow, together with Levites, Asaph's singers and gatekeepers. Temple servants and descendants of Solomon's servants also receive their place. These groups help explain what a restored community would need. A building alone would not provide worship, guarding, song or daily assistance. The return included people connected with all those duties, alongside the households whose fields and towns would sustain the wider life of Judah. Preserving their names recognises service that could otherwise disappear behind the better known story of rebuilding the sanctuary.~Some returnees could not demonstrate their family descent through the available records. Certain men claiming priestly ancestry searched for their registrations but did not find them. They were excluded from the priestly service and from the most holy food until the question could receive an authorised resolution. The governor did not simply declare the missing evidence irrelevant because workers were needed. Nor does the chapter supply a final verdict that it has not recorded. The restriction concerned a particular sacred responsibility. The account places hope and careful discernment together: the community could welcome a return from exile while still taking seriously the duties for which clear family qualification was required.~The whole assembly numbered forty-two thousand three hundred sixty, besides servants and the additional singers noted in the record. Horses, mules, camels and donkeys are also counted, reminding the reader of the practical scale of relocation. On reaching the site of God's house, some family heads gave freely according to their ability for its rebuilding. Gold, silver and priestly garments were added to the work's resources. Then people settled in their towns. The list ends in places of ordinary life rather than leaving everyone permanently gathered at the temple site. Worship would rise within a people who needed homes, livelihood, shared service and a continuing memory of who had come. Their restoration was made up of many lives, and the record treated those lives as worth remembering.|God's work includes households and varied forms of service. 3:1-13|The Altar Stands Before the Temple Is Built|Joy and weeping rise over a new foundation|When the seventh month arrived, the people gathered in Jerusalem with a shared purpose. Jeshua and the priests, together with Zerubbabel and his fellow leaders, rebuilt the altar of Israel's God. Their aim was to offer the sacrifices prescribed through Moses. Fear of surrounding peoples remained, but it did not postpone worship until every danger had gone. They set the altar on its base and began morning and evening burnt offerings. The people who had returned to a broken city could not yet point to a restored temple, but they could begin obeying what God had already made clear.~They kept the Feast of Tabernacles according to the written directions, supplying the daily offerings in their proper numbers. Regular sacrifices, new moons, appointed festivals and voluntary gifts followed. The first day of the seventh month marked this renewed service, even though the temple foundation had not been laid. The sequence matters. They did not possess every structure they hoped to build before drawing near through the worship appointed to them. Meanwhile, masons and carpenters received money, and food, drink and oil went to workers in Sidon and Tyre. Cedar would be brought from Lebanon by sea to Joppa under Cyrus's permission. Present worship and future preparation proceeded together.~In the second month of the second year after their arrival, leaders, priests, Levites and the returning people began work on the house. Levites from twenty years old supervised. When the builders laid the foundation, the priests stood with trumpets and Asaph's descendants with cymbals, following David's arrangements. They sang in response, praising the Lord for His goodness and enduring mercy towards Israel. The people shouted because the foundation was laid. Their rejoicing concerned a beginning rather than a finished achievement. After the loss and interruption of exile, even the first visible stage of rebuilding gave them reason to thank God.~Among the older priests, Levites and family heads were people who remembered the former temple. Seeing the new foundation, they wept loudly while others shouted for joy. The sounds mingled until listeners could not distinguish the weeping from the celebration, and the noise carried far. The chapter does not require the reader to invent one motive for every tear. It records memory, grief and hope meeting within the same assembly. Those starting again could receive the present gift while still feeling the weight of what had been lost. The new foundation did not erase the old sorrow. It gave a community carrying that sorrow a place from which to continue worshipping and working together.|Begin the obedience already within reach, even while much remains unfinished. 4:1-24|Opposition Grows from Discouragement to Official Accusation|The rebuilding faces pressure across successive reigns|When the adversaries of Judah and Benjamin heard that the returned exiles were building the temple, they approached Zerubbabel and the family heads. They asked to share the work, claiming to seek the same God and recalling their settlement under Esarhaddon of Assyria. Zerubbabel, Jeshua and the other leaders refused the proposed partnership. They would build together under the charge given by Cyrus. The account identifies the approaching group as adversaries before recording their offer. Its concern is the responsibility for rebuilding God's house, and the leaders would not surrender that responsibility merely because the proposal sounded cooperative.~The surrounding people then weakened the builders' hands, frightened them and hired advisers to frustrate the undertaking. Pressure continued through the days of Cyrus towards Darius's reign. The chapter broadens its account by including accusations from later reigns, under Ahasuerus and Artaxerxes, showing the continuing pattern of opposition to Jerusalem. In the letter associated with Artaxerxes, Rehum, Shimshai and their companions accused the Jews of rebuilding a rebellious city and its walls. These city accusations should be distinguished from the temple work with which the chapter begins. The collected correspondence shows how opponents used political suspicion across time, before the narrative returns to the earlier interruption of the temple project.~The writers warned that a restored city would stop paying taxes and damage the king's revenue. They professed concern for royal honour and asked for a search of earlier records. Jerusalem's past rebellions, they argued, proved that rebuilding threatened imperial control beyond the Euphrates. Their letter took facts about a troubled history and used them to frame the present people as an inevitable danger. The king answered that records confirmed former resistance and powerful rulers in Jerusalem. He ordered the building of the city stopped until a further command. Rehum and his companions hurried to enforce the instruction, using force and power to make the Jews cease.~The final verse returns to the temple work, which remained stopped until Darius's second year. This ending leaves the reader facing a real delay. Cyrus's decree had been genuine, the people had returned and the altar stood, yet progress was not uninterrupted. Opposition used fear, discouragement, hired influence and official procedure. Faithfulness would require more than the enthusiasm of laying the first foundation. The chapter prepares for the renewed prophetic call that follows, while warning against a simple expectation that God's work never meets obstruction. It also asks careful readers to follow the different subjects and periods in the correspondence rather than flatten every accusation into one moment or one building project.|Discouragement and misrepresentation can obstruct good work. 5:1-17|The Builders Resume and Answer the Governor|God's care sustains work under examination|Haggai and Zechariah spoke to the Jews in Judah and Jerusalem in the name of Israel's God. Their ministry led into action. Zerubbabel and Jeshua rose and began rebuilding the house, with the prophets beside them in support. The long interruption did not have to become the permanent condition of the work. God's word called the leaders back to their duty, and the prophets' help was more than distant encouragement. The account places them with the builders as the project resumed. Renewed obedience did not begin because every political question had already been settled. It began with a fresh response to what God had said.~Tattenai, governor of the province beyond the Euphrates, came with Shethar-boznai and other officials. They asked who had authorised the building and requested the names of those responsible. The questions could have led to another immediate halt. Instead, the elders continued while the matter was referred to Darius, because God's eye was upon them. The writer does not claim that the governor never examined their work. Divine care is shown within an examination that was allowed to proceed without stopping it. The builders had to answer real officials and trust God through the period before the royal response arrived.~Tattenai's report described a house being built with large stones and timber, progressing diligently. It included the elders' answer. They called themselves servants of the God of heaven and earth and explained that they were rebuilding a house originally erected by a great king of Israel. They also acknowledged why it had been destroyed. Their ancestors had provoked God, who gave them into Nebuchadnezzar's hand, leading to the temple's destruction and the people's exile. Their defence did not rest on pretending that Judah had an entirely innocent history. They confessed the judgment while distinguishing it from the present royal permission to rebuild.~They explained that Cyrus had ordered the house rebuilt and returned its gold and silver vessels through Sheshbazzar, whom he appointed governor. Sheshbazzar had laid the foundation, and the house remained unfinished. Tattenai asked Darius to search the archives to establish whether the decree existed and then send his ruling. The chapter ends with that request still before the king. Honest testimony, careful records and continuing work appear together under God's watch. The elders did not need to manufacture a more impressive past or threaten the official who questioned them. They could state both their failure and the mercy that had opened a new beginning. Their answer grounded present obedience in a history they were willing to tell truthfully.|Trust in God's care is consistent with answering questions honestly. 6:1-22|The Decree Is Found and the Temple Is Finished|An enquiry becomes provision for the work|Darius ordered a search of the archives. At Ecbatana in Media, a scroll was found preserving Cyrus's decree concerning God's house in Jerusalem. It authorised the rebuilding, described the construction and directed that royal funds meet the cost. It also required the temple vessels taken by Nebuchadnezzar to be returned to their places. The elders' answer had been supported by the written record. What their opponents or officials might have treated as a doubtful claim now stood before the king as an earlier royal instruction. The search did not invent permission. It recovered a command already given and still relevant to the unfinished house.~Darius instructed Tattenai and the other officials to leave the builders alone. He went further, ordering that the provincial revenue provide the expenses without delay. Animals, grain, salt, wine and oil needed by the priests were to be supplied daily so that sacrifices could be offered and prayers made for the king and his sons. The decree carried a severe imperial penalty against anyone who altered it. Those who had asked about authority now received orders to support the work. Tattenai and his companions complied promptly. The account shows a remarkable reversal through ordinary instruments of government: an archive, a recovered scroll and an enforceable letter.~The elders continued building under the encouragement of Haggai and Zechariah. The writer gathers God's command and the supporting decrees of Persian kings into the account of completion. The temple was finished on the third day of Adar in Darius's sixth year. Priests, Levites and returned exiles dedicated it with joy. They offered cattle, rams and lambs, together with twelve goats as a sin offering for all Israel, according to the number of the tribes. Their thanksgiving did not claim that rebuilding had made atonement unnecessary. They came as a restored people still needing forgiveness. Priests and Levites were arranged in their divisions for service according to Moses' book.~Passover followed on the fourteenth day of the first month. Priests and Levites had purified themselves, and the returning exiles shared the meal with others who had separated themselves from the surrounding people's impurity to seek Israel's God. This detail shows that belonging in the worshipping assembly involved allegiance to the Lord, not a simple claim that no outsider could ever approach. They kept Unleavened Bread for seven days with joy. God had turned the imperial ruler's heart towards them and strengthened the building of His house. The work begun amid tears and then delayed now supported gathered worship again. Completion became a reason to remember the Lord's help and continue the service for which the house had been rebuilt.|God can provide through decisions and records beyond our control. 7:1-28|Ezra Sets His Heart to Study, Practise and Teach|Learning God's law prepares a leader for service|After the temple's completion, the narrative moves to Artaxerxes's reign and introduces Ezra. His priestly line is traced through ancestors including Hilkiah, Zadok, Phinehas and Aaron. He was a skilled scribe in Moses' law, which God had given Israel. The king granted his request because the Lord's hand was upon him. Other Israelites, priests, Levites, singers, gatekeepers and temple servants went with him towards Jerusalem. The journey began in the first month and reached its destination in the fifth. The account explains Ezra's readiness through a settled purpose: he had prepared his heart to seek God's law, practise it and teach its commands in Israel.~Artaxerxes gave him a letter permitting willing Israelites and their ministers to join the journey. Ezra was commissioned to enquire into Judah and Jerusalem according to God's law. He carried gold and silver freely offered by the king, counsellors and people for the temple service. Animals, grain and drink offerings were to be purchased, and remaining resources used according to God's will. Vessels were to be delivered to the house, with further needs supplied from the treasury. The letter gave Ezra substantial opportunity, but his competence could not be reduced to possessing royal authority. He carried a law he had determined to obey before he required others to learn it.~Provincial treasurers were instructed to supply specified amounts of silver, wheat, wine and oil, with salt as needed. Temple servants received exemption from certain taxes. Ezra was also authorised to appoint magistrates and judges for those governed by God's law and to teach those who did not know it. The decree included civil penalties for disobedience to divine and royal law. These powers belonged to the Persian administration described in the account. They show the breadth of Ezra's commission, extending beyond the delivery of donations to instruction and judgment within the restored community. Such authority made personal wisdom and faithfulness more necessary, not less.~Ezra answered the opportunity with praise. He blessed the God of his fathers for placing the desire to honour the Jerusalem temple in the king's heart and for granting him mercy before the court. He was strengthened because God's hand was upon him and gathered leading Israelites to accompany him. His thanksgiving did not pretend that the king and counsellors had played no part. It recognised God's kindness through their decisions. The chapter joins ancestry, study, obedience, teaching and gratitude in one servant's preparation. Ezra's heart had been directed towards the word before his influence expanded. The restored house now needed a people learning to live before the God whose name it bore.|Study and teaching should be joined to personal obedience. Ezra inspected the people and priests and found no Levites among them. He sent leaders and men of understanding to Iddo at Casiphia with a request for servants for God's house. Sherebiah and other Levites came, together with two hundred twenty temple servants. Ezra attributed their arrival to God's good hand. Careful preparation had exposed a real absence, and prayerful dependence included taking steps to supply the missing people.~At Ahava he proclaimed a fast so that the travellers could humble themselves and seek a safe journey for their children, possessions and company. Ezra had told the king that God's good hand rested on those who sought Him, and he felt ashamed to request a military escort against enemies along the road. The account describes his particular decision and its reason, rather than giving every later traveller a command to refuse protection. The company fasted and prayed, and God heard them. Their trust included concern for little ones who depended on decisions others made. Faith was expressed through openly acknowledging their need before setting out.~Ezra then entrusted the precious metals and vessels to appointed priests and Levites. Silver, gold and costly bronze were weighed into their hands. He reminded them that both they and the vessels were set apart to the Lord and that the wealth had been freely offered to Israel's God. They were to guard it until it could be weighed again before the responsible leaders in Jerusalem. Trust did not remove accountability. The gifts came from the king, counsellors, officials and Israelites, and their intended purpose required protection through the journey. After three days, the treasures were weighed into the hands of temple officials, with their numbers and weights written down. The travellers offered sacrifices for all Israel and presented the royal commissions to the provincial authorities, who supported the people and God's house. 9:1-15|Ezra Hears of Unfaithfulness and Kneels in Confession|Restored buildings cannot excuse a returning pattern of sin|After the journey and delivery of gifts, leaders brought Ezra disturbing news. Israelites, including priests and Levites, had entered marriages joining them to the surrounding peoples and their detestable practices. Leaders had taken a prominent part. The report concerns the restored covenant community's disobedience and the danger of renewed idolatry. Ezra's response was grief, not satisfaction that he could expose other people's failure. He tore his garments, pulled hair from his head and beard and sat appalled. Those who trembled at God's words gathered around him. Until the evening sacrifice he remained overwhelmed, recognising how deeply the report challenged the meaning of the restoration.~At the evening offering Ezra rose, knelt in his torn garments and spread his hands before God. He confessed shame at the people's guilt, which had risen beyond them. His prayer repeatedly used the language of shared responsibility. From their ancestors' days, sin had brought rulers, priests and people under foreign powers, leading to sword, captivity, plunder and humiliation. Ezra did not describe the exile as an unfortunate event without a moral history. He remembered the very lesson the community was in danger of forgetting. His grief joined the present report to the long record of unfaithfulness that had already cost Israel so much.~He then recalled the mercy they had received. For a little time God had granted grace, leaving a remnant and giving them a firm place in His holy dwelling. Though still under foreign rule, they had not been abandoned. Persian kings had shown favour, allowing the temple to rise and providing renewed life and protection in Judah and Jerusalem. Ezra's words about a wall belong within this language of restored security and should not be made into a claim that every city defence had already been rebuilt. The central confession is clear: God's generosity had given them room to live again, and they were answering that mercy with renewed disobedience.~Ezra asked what they could say after abandoning commands given through God's servants. He recalled the warning against alliances with peoples whose practices had defiled the land and endangered Israel's faithful life across generations. God had punished them less than their guilt deserved and allowed this remnant to escape. To repeat the old course after such deliverance threatened the very community mercy had preserved. The prayer ends with God's righteousness and the people's guilt still before Him. There is no excuse, demand or confident claim that their restored temple guarantees safety. He proposed a covenant to put away the foreign wives and children according to the counsel of Ezra and those who feared God's command. He urged Ezra to act and promised support. The account records a grave proposal affecting whole households. Its seriousness must not disappear behind a general lesson about making a fresh start. The wrongdoing had already involved families, and the chosen response would involve wives and children whose lives were bound to those decisions.~Ezra required the leaders, priests, Levites and people to swear to carry out the undertaking. On the twentieth day of the ninth month, the men of Judah and Benjamin gathered in the temple square. They trembled because of the matter and the heavy rain. Ezra called them to confess and separate from the relationships identified as unfaithful. The assembly agreed, while recognising that the number of cases and the weather made an immediate settlement impossible.~They proposed appointed hearings, with local elders and judges accompanying those involved.
