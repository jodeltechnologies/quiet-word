1:1-10|An Opening That Goes Straight to the Trouble|Holding to the gospel received|After greeting the churches, Paul says he is astonished at their movement towards a different gospel. Some are troubling them and distorting the message of Christ. He says that even he or an angel would stand condemned for proclaiming a different message. He asks whether his aim is to please people or serve Christ.|Paul puts himself under the same test as any other messenger. The identity of a speaker does not settle whether a message is faithful.|Whom does Paul include in his warning about proclaiming a different gospel?
1:11-24|The Persecutor Becomes a Preacher|Grace that changes an established direction|Paul recalls his former zeal and the harm he did to the church. God called him by grace and revealed his Son so Paul would preach among the Gentiles. He describes where he travelled and when he met Peter and James. The churches of Judea heard that the former persecutor now preached the faith he once attacked, and they glorified God.|Paul acknowledges the harm of his earlier life. The story he tells gives the churches reason to glorify God for the direction in which grace turned him.|What was the report about Paul that reached the churches of Judea?
2:1-10|Titus Goes With Paul to Jerusalem|Fellowship without adding a burden|Paul goes to Jerusalem with Barnabas and Titus and sets the message he preaches before recognised leaders. Titus, a Greek, is not compelled to be circumcised. The leaders recognise Paul's calling among the Gentiles and give him and Barnabas the right hand of fellowship. They ask him to remember the poor, which he is eager to do.|The meeting recognises distinct fields of work and a shared fellowship. Remembering the poor remains a point of practical agreement.|What do the leaders recognise about Paul's work, and what request do they make?
2:11-21|Peter Leaves the Table|Living consistently with justification by faith|At Antioch Peter eats with Gentile believers. When certain people arrive, he withdraws out of fear, and others follow. Paul confronts him publicly because his conduct conflicts with the gospel. Paul explains that a person is justified through faith in Christ, and speaks of his own life as lived by faith in the Son of God who loved and gave himself for him.|Peter's retreat affects other people and puts a new condition on fellowship in practice. Paul connects the shared table with the ground on which believers are accepted.|What changed Peter's conduct, and why did Paul regard it as a gospel issue?
3:1-14|The Question About Receiving the Spirit|Beginning and continuing by faith|Paul asks the Galatians how they received the Spirit and whether they now expect to finish by human effort. He recalls Abraham believing God and the promise of blessing to the nations. He explains the curse of failing to do all the law and speaks of Christ redeeming people so the blessing and promise of the Spirit might reach them.|The question turns the Galatians towards how their new life began. Paul uses Abraham to show that receiving by faith belongs inside God's long purpose.|What experience does Paul ask the Galatians to remember before speaking about Abraham?
3:15-29|The Promise, the Law and the Heirs|Belonging to Christ across familiar boundaries|Paul argues that the law given later does not cancel God's earlier promise. He explains its temporary role, using the image of a guardian until Christ. Through faith believers are God's children and have put on Christ. Differences such as Jew and Greek, slave and free, male and female cannot take away their shared standing as heirs in him.|Paul's argument about the timing of the promise reaches real differences in the congregation. Their inheritance rests on belonging to Christ.|What role does Paul give the law in relation to the promise and to the coming of Christ?
4:1-20|An Heir Learns to Call God Father|Receiving sonship and caring for others|Paul compares an underage heir with a servant under guardians. When the time comes, God sends his Son and gives believers adoption. The Spirit of the Son cries to the Father. Paul then asks why the Galatians are turning back to bondage. He recalls their former welcome and says he longs for Christ to be formed in them.|Paul's concern for the churches is personal. His image of adoption gives them a reason to face the habits that pull them back towards servitude.|How does Paul describe the difference in the heir's relationship to the household after the appointed time?
4:21-31|Hagar and Sarah in Paul's Argument|Understanding how Paul uses the promise|Paul recalls Abraham's two sons, one born to a slave woman and the other to a free woman. He explicitly uses the account as an allegory for two covenants and develops the contrast between bondage and promise. He quotes Isaiah and identifies believers with the children of promise, like Isaac.|Paul tells the reader what kind of use he is making of the Genesis account. Following his argument carefully keeps the lesson tied to the freedom and promise he is discussing.|Which features of the two births does Paul use to develop his comparison?
5:1-15|Freedom Put to Work Through Love|Serving without returning to bondage|Paul urges the Galatians to stand in the freedom Christ has given. He warns of relying on circumcision for justification and asks what interrupted their obedience. Then he explains what freedom is for: serving one another in love. He recalls the command to love a neighbour and warns against consuming one another in quarrels.|Paul treats freedom as an opportunity for service. The life of the congregation will show whether people use that freedom for their neighbour's good.|What use of freedom does Paul recommend when people might use it for themselves?
5:16-26|What Grows When the Spirit Leads|Recognising character by its fruit|Paul describes the conflict between the flesh and the Spirit. He names actions that damage people and fellowship, including enmity, jealousy and drunkenness. Then he describes the Spirit's fruit, such as love, joy, peace, gentleness and self-control. He calls those who live by the Spirit to walk accordingly and avoid provoking or envying one another.|Fruit can be observed in relationships over time. Paul's closing words give people a place to examine whether their conduct is consistent with the Spirit whose life they profess.|Which examples of the flesh and the Spirit's fruit would show up first in a disagreement?
6:1-18|Bearing Burdens and Sowing for the Future|Gentleness, endurance and the cross|Paul tells those who are spiritual to restore a person caught in wrongdoing with gentleness and self-watchfulness. He calls believers to bear one another's burdens, examine their own work and persist in doing good. With his closing words he warns about the desire to make a public show and names Christ's cross as his ground for boasting.|Gentle restoration takes another person's burden seriously while recognising our own vulnerability. Paul's picture of sowing gives patient goodness a longer horizon than immediate results.|How does Paul join the duty to help another person with responsibility for one's own conduct?
